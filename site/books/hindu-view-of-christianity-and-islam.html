Hindu View of Christianity and Islam

by Ram Swarup · Voice of India · preface dated 27 September 1992

Western scholars studied Islam from a Christian point of view, and Hindus long saw their own religion through Christian and Islamic eyes. Swarup reverses the angle and looks at both religions from the standpoint of Hindu yoga. His conclusion is that Christianity and Islam are close cousins, which explains why they fought so hard. He calls them “prophetic” religions: one jealous God who speaks through one chosen messenger. Hindu spirituality, in his account, is “yogic”: God is found inside oneself and can appear in many forms.

About this guide. The book is a polemical comparison of religions by one of Voice of India's founders. This page sets out his argument in his own terms and attributes it to him. It does not endorse his judgements about any faith.

The core contrast · chapters 2 and 3

Two kinds of religion

The whole book rests on this contrast. Swarup groups Judaism, Christianity and Islam as “prophetic” religions, and Hinduism, Buddhism and the old pagan traditions as “yogic” ones. Each row is one point on which he says they differ.

“A yogic God is a unity, not a unit.”Swarup, contrasting the advaitic God of the Yogas and Puranas with the single God of the Bible and the Quran
Chapters 1 and 3 · one family

The family of prophetic creeds, as Swarup draws it

For Swarup the quarrel between Christianity and Islam is a family quarrel, “like Stalinists and Trotskyites.” Both took the jealous God of the Old Testament and narrowed revelation to a single messenger. Later prophets and some modern ideologies, in his view, repeat the same pattern. Hover over a name to see why he places it here.

Chapter 1 · how the West studied Islam

From crusade to scholarship

Swarup argues that Western study of Islam began as a weapon. When the crusades failed, the Church turned to languages and debate. Missionary scholars, many working in India, went on to write the classic lives of Muhammad.

Chapter 3 · where gods come from

Five planes of the mind

Swarup takes this scheme from Vyasa's commentary on the Yoga Sutras. Vyasa says samadhi, deep absorption, can happen on any plane of the mind. Only on the two highest planes is it yogic. Swarup argues that a god met in absorption on a lower, desire-driven plane comes out jealous and intolerant, and that the “prophetic” God is that kind of god.

Vyasa names the planes. Swarup's terms for the lower ones are “kama-bhumi” (plane of desire) and “krodha-bhumi” (plane of anger), as against the “yoga-bhumi”.

Chapter 2 · a world looking for its old gods

Who Swarup thinks Hinduism can help

Swarup quotes the American writer Ralph Borsodi: outside Asia Minor, the Semitic religions are “intruders.” He believes peoples converted long ago are now looking for the gods they lost. Because Hinduism kept the old “language of many Gods”, he argues it can help them find their way back.

Europe

Britain's Pagan Federation, which met in 1991, sees European paganism as a western branch of the same tradition as Hinduism: nature-venerating, polytheist, honouring the Goddess.

The Americas

Indigenous peoples still under church tutelage. Swarup says orthodox and liberation-theology Christianity “are sides of the same coin.”

Egypt and Iran

Tawfiq al-Hakim pairing Pharaonic Egypt with Hindu India; Iran's pride in its “Aryan” past held down, Swarup says, by its clergy.

Africa

Under missionary and Islamic pressure. Swarup asks why the UN protects the right to convert but not the right of tolerant cultures to resist conversion.
