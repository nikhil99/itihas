Defence of Hindu Society

by Sita Ram Goel · Voice of India · sequel to Hindu Society Under Siege

In the earlier book Goel described the siege. Here he sets out the defence. His first principle: Hindus should stop measuring their heritage by the standards of Christianity, Islam, Communism and modernism, and start measuring those by Hindu standards. Most of the book makes the case for what he calls universal spirituality: many gods, the inner quest and yoga. He contrasts it with monotheism, which he calls theology rather than spirituality.

Chapter 2 · Sanatana Dharma versus prophetic creeds

The verse everyone quotes a quarter of

Modern Hindus often cite “ekam sad viprā bahudhā vadanti” to show the Rigveda is monotheist at heart. Goel prints the whole verse, Rigveda 1.164.46. Most of it names gods: Indra, Mitra, Varuna, Agni, Garutman, Yama, Matarishvan. He reads the habit of quoting only the third quarter, and stressing its first word, as a “psychology of surrender”: asking to be admitted into monotheism's club.

1
indraṃ mitraṃ varuṇam agnim āhurThey call him Indra, Mitra, Varuna, Agni,
2
atho divyaḥ sa suparṇo garutmānand he is the heavenly, noble-winged Garutman.
3
ekaṃ sad viprā bahudhā vadantiThe one that exists, the wise speak of in many ways:
4
agniṃ yamaṃ mātariśvānam āhuḥthey call it Agni, Yama, Matarishvan.

Chapters 3 and 7 · the spiritual centre

Starting from man, not from God

Goel says Hindu seers began with the human self and tested what they found against direct experience, not against an assumed Almighty. He describes three “sublimities” of Sanatana Dharma and an inner path any seeker can take. He grounds it in the Upanishadic map of the self.

The five koshas (sheaths), outermost to innermost, from the Upanishads as Goel presents them. Hover over a ring.

Sublimity 1 · the aspiration

Every soul has an inborn hunger for truth, goodness, beauty and power (satyam, shivam, sundaram, aishvarya). It does not wait for any prophet.

Sublimity 2 · the goal

Mastery of oneself, and with it of the universe: “I have known whatever is to be known,” in Goel's paraphrase of the Buddha.

Sublimity 3 · open to all

The fulfilment is promised to every being, by an inner opening rather than an outer baptism.

Chapter 4 · Hindu spirituality versus monotheism

A world full of gods

Goel calls it “an intuition ingrained in the Hindu psyche” to see gods throughout the environment: in the sky, the elements, living things and the home. Missionaries called this pantheism and Western sociology called it animism. Goel treats it as the natural expression of a universal spirituality.

SkyThe firmament and space itself. Home of the Sun, Moon and stars, and of Dawn and Dusk.
AirStorm and breeze, and the breath that keeps everything alive.
EarthA Goddess of patience. Mountains, forests and sacred plants.
WaterRain, rivers and lakes. The Ganga and Godavari are Goddesses, Manasarovara is sacred.
FireSun, hearth and vital heat. Messenger of the gods at every sacrifice.
CreaturesMounts of the gods: Garuda for Vishnu, the bull for Shiva, the lion for Durga, the mouse for Ganapati, the swan for Sarasvati, the owl for Lakshmi. The cow above all.
Home“The mother is a Goddess and the father a God.”
Chapter 6 · revival of universal spirituality

Gods lost and gods still worshipped

Goel and Ram Swarup, whom he quotes at length, hold that every ancient nation once worshipped many gods. Where Christianity or Islam arrived, those gods were eclipsed. They argue that Hindu and Buddhist worship is the surviving link, and could help others bring their old gods back.

Chapter 9 · character of Nehruvian secularism

Two histories of secularism

In Europe

Until the mid-18th century the state was the Church's “secular arm.” Secularism arose in the 19th century to free the state and society from religious fanaticism.

In India, as Goel tells it

The Hindu state always supported every sect, and India had no theocracy until Islam arrived in the 8th century. In his account Nehruvian secularism kept the European name but became a shield for Islamic revivalism and an attack on “Hindu communalism”.

His verdict: “a Hindu cannot be a communalist in his own homeland.” He compares the secularist to the thug in a folk tale who convinces a Brahmin that the calf he is carrying is a dog.

Chapter 10 · the trap and the way out

Trapped by its own slogan

The trap, in Goel's view, is sarva-dharma-samabhava, “all religions are equal”. The Hindu elite applies it to creeds that do not return the courtesy, and any Hindu who questions it is shunned. His way out comes in two stages.

The minimum task

Treat Christianity and Islam as political ideologies that arrived with conquering armies, not as religions. Bring back into the Hindu fold those who were “forced or lured” out of it.

The maximum task

Take the argument abroad, to the creeds' own homelands, and help other peoples recover their old spirituality.

He also proposes a “rescue operation”: separating Christian mysticism from Christian theology and Sufism from prophetic Islam. He sees that as the only basis for Hindus to come to terms with Indian Christians and Muslims.
