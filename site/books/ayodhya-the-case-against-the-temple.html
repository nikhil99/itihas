Ayodhya: The Case Against the Temple

by Koenraad Elst · Voice of India · essays written 1995–2002

The title is ironic. Elst examines the “case against the temple”: the scholars who argued, from 1989 on, that no Hindu temple stood where the Babri Masjid was built. He concludes that they never produced positive evidence. In his account they relied on arguments from silence and on attacking a weakened version of the other side's case. The rest of the book goes after a related claim, that Hindus were themselves temple-breakers, case by case.

The site, as the book tells it

Ramkot hill, from the 11th century to 1998

Elst's starting point: until 1989 nearly everyone agreed a temple had stood here, including local Muslims, European travellers, British judges and the Encyclopaedia Britannica. He argues that the burden of proof should have been on those who wanted to overturn that consensus.

Chapters 1 and 11 · the scholars' debate

The government-sponsored debate, 1990–91

The government asked scholars nominated by the VHP and by the Babri Masjid Action Committee (BMAC) to exchange evidence. This is Elst's account of what each side submitted and how the exchange ended.

The VHP-nominated scholars' case
  • One hypothesis. The site was venerated as Rama's birthplace, a temple stood there, and it was demolished for the mosque.
  • Foundations. B.B. Lal's 1975–80 dig found 11th-century burnt-brick pillar bases aligned with the mosque.
  • Reused pillars. 14 black-stone temple pillars set into the mosque.
  • Testimony. European travellers and local Muslim writers from the 17th century on.
  • Pattern. Consistent with other cases such as the Gyanvapi mosque, Quwwat-ul-Islam and Adhai-din-ka-Jhonpra.
The BMAC scholars' case, as Elst reads it
  • Court records. Mostly British-era suits. Elst says they show possession, not that no temple existed.
  • Opinion pieces. Newspaper articles and book excerpts.
  • Arguments from silence. Tulsidas and the Ain-i-Akbari (1598) do not mention a demolition.
  • “British concoction”. The idea that the story was invented under divide-and-rule, which Elst calls “a figment of the imagination”.
  • No alternative. No document showing what else stood on the hill.
“The facts will remain the facts.”Elst, closing his 1998 paper prepared for the World Archaeological Congress
Chapters 2–6 · “Hindus did it too”

The counter-claims Elst disputes

A common reply to accounts of Islamic iconoclasm is that Hindu kings also destroyed Buddhist and Jain shrines. Elst takes the usual examples one at a time.

CaseThe claimElst's answer
Pushyamitra ShungaThe general who ended the Maurya dynasty (2nd c. BC) persecuted Buddhists.The story comes from the Divyavadana, a Buddhist text written centuries later, and there is no contemporary evidence for it.
Harsha of KanaujThe 7th-century king persecuted a religion.Bana's Harshacharita describes no persecution, only general harmony.
Harsha of Kashmir (r. 1089–1111)Kalhana records Harsha looting and desecrating temples.Kalhana himself calls him “the Turk king Harsha”. Kashmir had Turkish mercenaries after 1034, and Harsha plundered Hindu temples, so Elst sees him as following an Islamic pattern rather than a Hindu one.
Bodh GayaHindus took over the Mahabodhi temple.Buddhists left after the Muslim massacre of c. 1192. Shaiva monks kept the temple from 1590 and agreed to its restoration in 1874.
Richard Eaton's list“Only eighty” temple desecrations by Muslim rulers.The list is not exhaustive, and single entries cover many temples. One Benares entry stands for “nearly one thousand temples”.
Aurangzeb at KashiThe Vishvanath demolition was an “isolated” political act.His court chronicle records demolitions as policy. See the chart below.

Chapters 9, 12 and 13 · where Elst lands

Elst's conclusions

On the historians

Elst predicts the no-temple argument of the 1990s will be studied as “a remarkable case study” in politicised scholarship. He says foreign scholars adopted it without seeing the evidence.

On the court

In 1994 the Supreme Court declined to answer the single question referred to it: whether a temple had stood on the site. Elst argues the evidence debate had already answered it.

Against the “Indonesian model”

He rejects RSS chief K.S. Sudarshan's hope that Indian Muslims could become like Ramayana-loving Indonesian Muslims. He says even in Indonesia that kind of Islam is declining.

No more Ayodhyas

Elst advises Hindus not to turn their critique of Islam into disputes over temple sites. The lasting gain of Ayodhya, he says, was the debate over how history is written.
