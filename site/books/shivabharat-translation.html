Shivabharat in English

Kavindra Paramananda · Sanskrit, 17th century · translated from S.M. Divekar's edition (Pune, 1927)

The whole poem, all 32 cantos and 2,264 verses, from Maloji's day to the summer of 1661, where the only manuscript breaks off.

About this translation. It is a plain prose rendering, made verse by verse from the Sanskrit in Divekar's 1927 edition. His Marathi translation was used to check readings and proper names. Verse numbers follow his edition; where he groups verses (for example 24–27), the English keeps them together. The poet uses Sanskrit forms for names: Shiva for Shivaji, Shah for Shahaji, Jija for Jijabai, and "copper-faced men" for the Mughals. The translation keeps these forms. Where Divekar identifies a Sanskritised place or family name, it is given in its usual Marathi form, such as Bhosle, Pune or Chakan. Like the poem itself, the translation keeps the court poet's view: Shivaji is Vishnu come to earth, and the Muslims are demons. Read it as a near-contemporary insider's account, not as neutral history. This is a new translation, and some readings of the scan are uncertain.

Shahaji's birth

Salutation to Shri Ganesha. Salutation to Shri Samba Sadashiva. Salutation to the goddesses Shri Mahakali, Mahalakshmi and Mahasaraswati. May it be well.

1Again and again I bow to Govinda, whose lotus feet shine with the garlands of mandara flowers worn on the bowed heads of the greatest gods.

2Once upon a time Paramananda, an excellent Brahmin, went to the city of Varanasi on a pilgrimage.

3–4There, where all beings are released yet release itself is never released, where the Lord himself teaches the saving Brahman, this Brahmin who knew his duty performed the pilgrim's rites, beheld the great god Maheshvara, and sat down on the holy bank of the Bhagirathi.

5–7He sat there in the lotus posture, blazing with the radiance of Brahman: a knower of all the shastras, famous, learned in the inner science, foremost of the reciters of the Puranas, son of Bhatta Govinda, gifted with mastery of speech by the grace of the goddess Ekavira — Paramananda, prince of poets, bliss in bodily form. When they saw him the learned men who lived in Kashi were filled with joy.

8He rose to meet those noble men, greeted them, and honoured them with due ceremony as guests arriving at his home.

9Those best of the twice-born sat down around him, wishing to hear the famous story of King Shiva.

10Then all those wise men, delighted, said to him, the maker of fine poems and an incarnation of Brihaspati:

The learned men said:

11"The king who rules this earth, lord of the kings of the mountains, who won his kingdom by the grace of Tulaja, great in austerity;

12"born of a portion of Vishnu, and especially of the guardians of the world; high-minded, clear of soul, valiant, master of his senses;

13"terrible even to the terrible, the boundary-mark of all who bear the bow; wise, noble in conduct, glorious, of wondrous prowess;

14"accomplished, grateful, virtuous, self-perfected, bearing the marks of fulfilment; a speaker of true speech and a hearer of great discernment;

15"protector of gods, Brahmins and cows, destroyer of the wicked, guardian of those who take refuge, doer of good to his subjects —

16"the history of this king, made in many cantos, which you have brought to light by the grace of the goddess,

17"rich in noble diction, full of meaning, wonderful, endowed with sweetness and the other virtues of style and adorned with figures of speech,

18"filled with the meaning of the shastras of dharma and of statecraft, heard of in every land, a new Purana as it were —

19"— that whole history, free of every fault and possessed of every mark of excellence, tell to us, O knower of all, whose vows are praised."

20Thus entreated by the circle of wise men living in Varanasi, the righteous prince of poets, best of speakers, replied.

The prince of poets said:

21–22Bowing to the blessed Ekavira, to Ganesha and Sarasvati, and to my true guru, the great adept who grants success even to the adepts, I shall tell the history of the wise great king Shiva, as the Bharata tells the history of Bharata's line.

23Wise men should hear the glories of King Shiva, which take away the sins of the Kali age and captivate the hearts of men.

24–27This hero, lord of the mountains, triumphs: Shiva, the great king of the South, son of King Shahaji, Narayana himself made manifest, the splitter of the Muslims' pride. Once, graciously, he said to me, who am devoted to him: "Whatever deeds I have done on earth, and whatever I am doing, describe them all, wise one. Beginning with my grandfather, the famous King Maloji, set out this great story, O fortunate one."

28–30Welcoming those words of his, O best of the twice-born, I promised, came home, and thought to myself: "Ah, how shall I make a great work like the Bharata, on this wonder of a life of Shiva?" As I pondered thus with a steady mind for a long while, the blessed goddess herself came to me and said:

The goddess said:

31"Prince of poets, do not worry. I am on your side. It was by my command that this king laid this task on you.

32Comforting me in this way, the compassionate goddess of our family, the blessed four-armed one, entered my heart.

33From that moment, as I watched, the whole of the Word, Brahman as speech, came alive together with its meaning and settled on the tip of my tongue.

34Seeing plainly before me all things past and to come, I became like a being more than human.

35Then, thinking that I had done what I had to do, I made this sinless account of great merit.

36–41In it are told the greatness of Shambhu, the great god, and of Tulaja, slayer of the wicked demons; dharma, wealth, desire and liberation, each in its place; and the glory of the holy places, rightly set out. In it are the many wars of Shiva, who came down to earth to destroy the Muslims, against those Muslims; the surpassing greatness of gods, Brahmins and cows; the pure and wonderful deeds of kings; the marks of elephants, horses and forts; and the everlasting science of kingship, set out in full. Listen with undivided minds, best of hearers, as I tell of that sinless line of the Sun from its beginning.

42–43In the southern quarter, in the line of the Sun, was born the glorious King Maloji, a lord of men bright as the sun itself. Clear of soul and bearer of the yoke of his own dharma, that lord of the Maharashtra land ruled the country of Maharashtra.

44Lotus-eyed, with the valour of the lord of Lakshmi, deep in virtue, he shone as he delighted his subjects.

45That righteous man made his home in the holy country of Pune and, by the Bhima river, made his kingdom more prosperous than his own.

46–47The banks of the Bhima were trampled by the hooves of his leaping horses; the roar of his kettledrums shook the ocean; his splendour scorched the circle of enemy kings. That mighty man by his own strength enjoyed the whole region of the Sahyadri.

48Unbearable to his enemies, foremost of all bowmen, born of a portion of Narayana, he was like a second Dhananjaya.

49He married with due rites the illustrious Uma, born of a great family, as Satyavan married Savitri.

50He prized her greatly, for she was endowed with beauty and virtue to match his own, as Aja, son of Raghu, prized the virtuous Indumati.

51As if it were a gift that Parvati had given of her own self, the virtuous lady adorned the name Uma.

52Then, rich as Kubera and blessed with that beautiful wife, the wise and accomplished king performed many kinds of righteous works.

53In his kingdom there were always sacred fires, houses of free food, sacrifices with ample fees to the priests, and great gifts.

54To please Shambhu, that devotee of Shambhu dug a lake of sweet water on the hill of Shambhu, as wide as an ocean.

55–56That righteous man built in many places mansions high as golden Meru with lofty gateways, delightful gardens rich in trees, long step-wells with flights of golden stairs, water-sheds and rest-houses.

57As the Ganga followed Bhagiratha, so a great and mighty army of four arms followed that strong man.

58Princes and kings bowed before that exalted king as reeds bow before the wind beside the ocean.

59–60At this time the righteous Nizam Shah ruled the earth, living at the fort of Devagiri. All the kings of the South, the Yadava king and the rest, always served that lord of the Muslims.

61At the same time Adil Shah too, a Muslim, ruled a kingdom, living with Muslims around him in the city called Vijaya.

62Then, after some time, for some reason, a great and violent quarrel arose between Adil Shah and the Nizam.

63Then the shrewd Nizam, hearing that King Maloji was as strong as Death, decided to seek his help.

64Then, to do what the Nizam wished, the unrivalled King Maloji went to Devagiri and lived there.

65His brother too, Vithoji Raje, terrible in valour like Bhima, joined him there with his army and waited on the lord of Dharagiri.

66Glad at heart that the two had come to him, the Nizam honoured them again and again with kind words and gifts.

67Whoever rose up as an enemy of the Nizam Shah, the strong-armed King Maloji rooted out.

68Vithoji Raje too, with the might of Shakra, always helped the Nizam and did what he wanted done.

69The Nizam had many helpers, yet Maloji stood above them all.

70Placing the kingdom inherited from his forefathers in the hands of his ministers, he governed another territory granted him by the Nizam Shah.

71Then, as he lived with his wife and longed for a son to make his royal fortune shine, many days went by.

72Wanting offspring, that king and his lawful wife took a great vow and worshipped Mahadeva, god of gods.

73Then, after a long time, the beloved queen of that mighty man conceived, to his joy.

74In the tenth month, at an auspicious hour, she happily gave birth to a wonderful son glowing with the marks of a king.

75–76Fine-nosed, wide-eyed, broad-browed, with smooth curling hair, broad-chested, long-armed, high-shouldered, golden in colour, with reddish lotus hands and feet, he lit up the house with his own radiance from the moment he was born.

77When they saw him the nurses were overjoyed and in their excitement sent word to the king through the servants of the inner palace.

78Hearing that great news of a son's birth, he felt as if every limb had been bathed in a shower of nectar.

79Then, eager and swimming in joy, the king came and gazed on the face of his tender young son.

80–83Then, delighted, he had the birth rites performed for him with blessings by his own family priest. When this prince, bright as Kumara, was born, auspicious instruments sounded, courtesans danced, singers with smooth and steady voices sang songs, bards loudly recited his titles, and the best of the twice-born gave their joyful blessings, which never fail. In house after house a great festival was held.

84–85To the people who came asking, the lord then gave great pearls, coral, jewelled ornaments, gold, cloth of gold, cattle, horses and elephants; and to them he seemed a wish-granting tree in human form.

86Then, on a fine day free of evil influences and named by the astrologers, the father himself named the boy Shah.

87Day by day the lotus-faced child grew, increasing the joy of his parents' eyes with his play.

88Then, when two years had passed, the king's wife bore a second son, like joy made flesh.

89For him too the learned performed the rites, and the lord gave him the name Sharif, as the holy men had declared.

90Those two boys, Shah and Sharif, named by the holy men, lamps of their family, grew up, and the family's fortune grew with them.

91Glad at heart, he and his kin rejoiced in those two sons who bore all the marks of kingship, and the lord felt that his royal line had put out new shoots on the earth.

92When a wise man hears this story of the birth of the splendid royal princes, famous in the world, which I have told, and which takes away the stain of the Kali age, he at once gains what he desires.

Thus ends the first canto, called "The Birth of the Princes", in the Samhita of a hundred thousand verses, the Anupurana of the line of the Sun, brought to light by Kavindra Paramananda.

The marriages of Shahaji and Sharifji

The prince of poets said:

1–2When Sharif's elder brother reached his fifth year, the great and noble King Maloji, taking up the bow he loved, set out with a great army for a campaign against Indrapura, sent by the Nizam.

3–5There, as he fought for his master and was attacked from every side by many strong warriors armed with clubs who struck at him again and again, he set flowing a great, swift river of blood that carried along foot soldiers, maddened elephants and horses. Then, enraged like Death, blazing with his own fierce energy, he sent the enemy warriors before him and set out himself on the road to heaven.

6Hearing that news, terrible as a thunderbolt, the virtuous Uma fell to the ground like a plantain tree struck by the wind.

7Bereft as the beauty of day is bereft of the sun, she sank by force, without support, into an ocean of darkness.

8–9Then King Vithoji, his eyes filled with tears, wishing to calm his brother's wife, who was so stricken, overwhelmed by grief for her husband and crying like an osprey, himself spoke to her in a voice choked with sobs, great-minded as he was:

10"Know that this world is always passing, noble lady, and give up your grief. Your husband has left his own people and gone to heaven.

11"This is the road heroes pray for, the heroes who never turn back: death in the forefront of battle from the blows of enemy weapons.

12"Those who want at once to drink nectar in the world of the gods, who look to their own goal, surely have no love for their own kin.

13"Alas! Your husband has abandoned you, virtuous lady, and your little children, and gone to the other world. His heart was hard.

14"Without doubt the bodies of the embodied are perishable. Even when guarded with care, they waste away when life runs out.

15–17"This body — anointed with fine unguents, decked with fine ornaments, dressed in soft and lovely clothes, fed on many kinds of delicacies to be eaten, licked, drunk and sucked, cared for with light, sweet and wholesome food, fragrant with the smoke of black aloewood incense, living in delightful mansions lit by bright jewelled lamps —

18–20"— laid to sleep on a lovely bed soft as shirisha flowers, fanned by fans cooled with spray, its feet pressed again and again by the lotus hands of beautiful women, kept alive with every effort and many remedies: this body, as if ungrateful, follows no one into the other world. So in this world no one truly belongs to anyone.

21–22"Loving wives, sons who bear good marks, mother and father, brothers born of one womb, friends and enemies, fortune and misfortune — these last only as long as the body lasts.

23"Here, O queen, are your two sons, Shah and Sharif. Full of energy, they shine like the sun and moon risen together.

24"Through these two jewels of the family your fame will be bright. In all the world you will be called the mother of heroes.

25"These two children, alas, still drinking milk, depend on you for their lives. Without you, how could they stay in this world even for a moment?

26"So hold on to your life for the sake of these two lives, and take courage now at these words of mine."

27Thus King Vithoji consoled his brother's wife, for the sake of the family's survival and above all of the kingdom's.

28Turning her eyes, in her love, on her two sons, that sinless queen put aside her grief and held on to life.

29Mastering even that great grief, the great-minded man performed for his brother all the rites for the other world.

30Then the valiant King Vithoji, putting Shah and Sharif forward, made the whole kingdom secure.

31When the Nizam heard of Maloji's death, he felt that his army was like a bird whose wings had been cut.

32–33Then the great-minded Nizam summoned Maloji's two sons, Shah and Sharif, together with King Vithoji, consoled them, himself gave them their father's estate, and honoured them with lovely golden ornaments and clothes.

34Pleased, the lord of Dharagiri also honoured them with priceless strings of jewels, elephants and horses, and sent the two of them home.

35Brought up by their father's brother, the two boys' bodies, still in childhood, took on the highest splendour.

36Then Sharif's elder brother, a home of every fortune and endowed with excellent marks, came to bear the title Raja.

37All the great lords, though he was still a boy, honoured that strong-armed son of Uma and bowed to him.

38As youth entered Shah's body, childhood shrank away little by little, as if crowded out.

39His face, with the line of a moustache just appearing, was as beautiful as a young elephant's brow marked with the first streak of rut.

40–41His face, golden and shining, with eyes stretched to the ears and a nose that frightened the parrots, was lovely and put the moon to shame. His arms reached to his knees and his body was wonderful.

42–44When King Jadhavrao saw Maloji's son Shah — great in generosity, great in compassion, great in war, of great energy, marked with excellent signs — then, on a day fixed by astrologers and marked by fine omens, he gave him his daughter Jija, a jewel of a girl in a noble house, lotus-eyed, a maiden of good family, of auspicious marks and marked for victory.

45Winning that great-souled man, she who had a radiant body and was deep in virtue shone like the Ganga reaching the ocean.

46–49And he, winning that beloved wife, like the lotus-handed goddess of earth's fortune — rich in beauty and splendour, of noble descent; her smooth dark curls falling to her hips; her brows like the bow of the god of love, set on the half-moon of her forehead; long-eyed; her ears like golden shells; her nose straight; her teeth like fresh jasmine buds; her lips red; her face a full-blown lotus; her throat like a conch; her voice like the cuckoo's; her arms soft as lotus stalks; in the bloom of new youth —

50–59— her hands like opening lotuses, her fingers playing like young shoots, her nails smooth and red, her navel deep, her waist slender, her thighs lovely as plantain stems, her ankles hidden, a famous lady; her feet marked with the signs of royalty, a home of every fortune; her smooth, glossy braid bound with many ornaments; the parting of her hair lined with rows of pearls and gems, her head bright with a red flower; golden earrings strung with pearls at their edges; a jewelled ornament on her forehead; lovely with a necklace of great pearls and gems, bearing clusters of jewels and pearls; armlets of jewels and gold, bracelets set with gems; her slender waist girdled with a jewelled belt; walking like a swan, with jingling jewelled anklets; her toes adorned with priceless jewelled rings; dressed in cloth of gold thread; in a bodice glittering with rows of pearls and gems — gazing at her bright face, himself bright-faced, that jewel of the solar line entered his home to the sound of music.

60As she came in with her husband, humble and dependent on him, she seemed like Shri entering the house with Narayana.

61The fortunate women of the town quickly gathered, gazing at her in wonder, and waved lamps before her.

62As she bowed, her mother-in-law greeted her with blessings that never fail and looked at her daughter-in-law with delight.

63–64The virtuous women praised her: devoted to serving her mother-in-law, dearer to her husband than anything, skilled in guarding her good name, modesty as if in bodily form, keeping to the bounds set by virtuous women, honoured in both families, bringing joy to her household.

65Sharif too, strong-armed, took as his wife Durga, the virtuous daughter of King Vishvasrao.

66Served by that slender daughter-in-law and by Jija, and with her two well-mannered sons, Uma found the highest joy.

67Those two brothers, of countless virtues, shining with two wives of boundless virtue, at once made the whole earth bright with their glory and made their own mother rejoice.

68With his brother, whose course was never checked, like Agni with the wind; fiercer in deed than Bhishma; victorious in battle after battle — the famous King Shah, of matchless strength, bow and arrows in hand, became, to do the Nizam's pleasure always, like Prithu, the ruler of the earth among all the kings.

Thus ends the second canto, called "The Marriages of Shah and Sharif", in the Samhita of a hundred thousand verses, the Anupurana of the line of the Sun, brought to light by Paramananda of Nidhivasa.

The Khandagale elephant affair; the Jadhav–Bhosale feud

The prince of poets said:

1–2Then, when King Vithoji, his father's brother, had also gone to heaven, the wise, steady and very brave King Shah, who followed the rules of kingship and obeyed his mother's word, took the great burden of the kingdom on his own arm.

3–4Sambhaji, Khelaji, Malaji, Mambaji, Nagoji, Parsoji, Tryambakji and Vakaji: these brothers born of one womb were the sons of King Vithoji, equal in valour to Indra. Shah and Sharif were the two mighty sons of King Maloji.

5–7These were eager to conquer lands, able in the work of war, doers of the Nizam's pleasure, with bows always in their hands, with bodies tall as mountains, bright as the thousand-rayed sun, made matchless in energy by the great strength of their arms, their large following, their army and other qualities. Following the counsel of Malik Ambar, they cared nothing for their enemies.

8–9One day the Nizam Shah came out of the inner palace with his trusted servants and ministers and sat on the throne in the hall of audience. Jadhavrao and all the others saw him, bowed in due order, and then quickly set off, each to his own lodging.

10As they left, those men who vied with one another in spirit, there was a great crush of people outside the gateway of the hall.

11–14There, with mace-bearers in front loudly clearing people from the way, went the kings: thick-shouldered and broad-chested, armed, wearing crowns and earrings, their bodies covered in coats of mail, bearing great necklaces of pearls and gems and armlets, of fine marks, lotus-eyed lords of the earth, each surrounded by his own troops, with tall banners carried before him. Some rode horses, some elephants, some went in litters, all of unchecked power, in that crowded place.

15Then an elephant at the head of a king named Khandagale broke loose by force and charged, trampling the troops of others.

16Though its driver checked it at every step with blows of the goad, that elephant wrought great slaughter among the soldiers.

17No one could stop that fearless beast as it crushed whole troops and roared like a cloud at the end of the world.

18Then Datta and the other sons of Jadhavrao could not bear its roaring, as one elephant cannot bear the roar of a rival.

19Then, at Datta's command, warriors struck that rut-maddened elephant with arrows, swords, lances, javelins and spears.

20–21Its armour torn by many warriors, that elephant of terrible deeds suddenly scattered the horsemen with its great trunk. Some it seized with its trunk and hurled away, some it threw down, and some it knocked down and crushed under its feet.

22Seeing the rout of his own troops, Dattaji went for that elephant like a lion fixing its eyes on its prey.

23Then that elephant, maddened by the weapons that had struck it hard, shook its head and trumpeted at the head of the battle.

24Then Sambhaji and Khelaji, the two sons of King Vithoji, came up to help Khandagale.

25Those two guarded the elephant, which had now come under Datta's power, its body smeared with blood, like a mountain streaked with red ochre.

26Then King Datta left that elephant with its broken trunk and attacked the strong-armed King Sambhaji.

27–29As those two fought a duel in anger, a great crowd of warriors came running, and then hand-to-hand fighting broke out between the two forces. As Datta charged Sambhaji, who shone like Indra, the lord King Shah, Sambhaji's brother, ignoring even his ties by marriage to the Yadavas, guarded Sambhaji's side.

30Then the valiant King Datta, shield in hand, whirled the tip of his sword around him and made a circle as if to fence himself in.

31In that sudden battle, the quarters were deafened by the shouts of the warriors who rushed in and the noise of slapped arms.

32Then that hero, making circles with the tip of his sword, danced on the stage of battle, as it were, in the greatest style.

33–37The crowned heads of the best warriors rolled on the ground; arms with their bows were cut through by swords; armour was split by blades, arrows, axes and lances; hands with their arrows were cut off by sharp discs; the dust of the battlefield was laid by streams of blood flowing from horses, rut-maddened elephants and their riders; the battlefield was covered with the cloth of banners; men fell with bodies full of arrows. Then that steady Yadava hero, slayer of enemies, meeting King Sambhaji, pierced the orb of the sun — he fell and went to the world of the sun.

38–39When the lord of the Yadavas, who had been going on ahead, heard that his own son Datta, matchless in deeds, had been killed by Sambhaji in battle, he turned back, his eyes red with anger, intent on killing King Sambhaji.

40When the lord of the Yadavas was seized by that rage, the earth with its mountains, forests and islands shook.

41"The wretch who killed my son, dearer to me than life itself — I will kill him and have my wish."

42Seeing his father-in-law, brave as a god, seized by anger in this way, King Shah, wishing to protect his own side, fought him.

43Seeing his son-in-law fighting, the bold enemy-tamer struck Shah on the arm, which was like the king of serpents.

44Struck by that sword, Shah fell into a deep faint, and only by his own steadiness did he somehow keep his life.

45–47Then, after overcoming Khelaji, humbling other kings and defeating the Nizam's army of black-faced men, that Yadava, firm of grip, raised his sword and in fury rushed at Sambhaji, unconquerable in battle. Then Sambhaji, serene as if he were laughing outright, quickly put his hand to the hilt of his own sword.

48Then a battle broke out between those two as they vied with each other, like two maddened elephants, which filled the hearts of others with fear.

49Then that Yadava, conqueror of enemies, after bearing many sword blows from Sambhaji, laid him on the ground with his own sword.

50Laying low on the ground the man who had killed his son, that Yadava hero took his revenge.

51–52Alas! When the great King Jadhavrao had laid low in battle the eldest son of King Vithoji, the Nizam's whole army could do nothing to answer it and lost heart.

53–54Then, when their master the Nizam had soothed them and checked them, the two armies, deeply troubled, drew back from their quarrel. Lifting from the battlefield the bodies of Sambhaji and Datta, and grieving, each went in despair to its own camp.

55Then all of Sambhaji's younger brothers, Khelkarna and the rest, sick at heart, mourned their eldest brother.

56Jadhavrao had the funeral rites performed for his son, and Khelkarna duly performed them for his eldest brother.

57When the Yadava, wise as he was, reflected in his proud heart on this battle, so contrary to their close ties by marriage, fought with rivals of the Bhosle line, we think he felt some remorse.

Thus ends the third canto, called "The Sudden Clash", in the Anupurana of the line of the Sun, brought to light by Kavindra Paramananda of Nidhivasa.

The rise of the Nizam Shahi

The prince of poets said:

1–3Then the Nizam, the master who sided with Khelkarna and the other sons of King Vithoji, men of wonderful deeds, knowing that the lord of the Yadavas — who had a large following, a very strong army and many troops, who destroyed his opponents like a second wielder of the thunderbolt — was very hard to restrain, plotted a great deceit in his heart.

4Learning of his wicked plan, that strong-armed leader of the Yadavas left and joined the lord of Delhi.

5When the lord of the Yadavas left the Nizam's country and went away, Adil Shah at once saw his chance and was glad.

6For he had long been humbled by the Nizam at every step. So he himself made a treaty with the lord of the copper-faced men.

7The noble and valiant lord of Delhi, a long-time rival of the Nizam, at once promised to do what Adil Shah wanted.

8Jahangir, lord of the copper-faced men, of very fierce valour, sent an army to help Ibrahim.

9Gaining that army of copper-faced men, Adil Shah thought his enemy the Nizam no more than a straw.

59As that proud man fought on in fury, they brought Sharif down with sharp arrows from every side.

62Then, his rut-maddened elephants driven back in terror by fine spears, the valiant copper-faced man turned and went back.

63As he withdrew from the battle with his elephants unharmed, the Nizam's soldiers raised lion-roars.

Then the general Ambar, with the Bhosles, bowed before the Nizam.

Shivaji's birth

The prince of poets said:

1Then Hari, lord of yogis, wishing himself to enter her womb, graciously showed himself to Shah's wife.

2–7Once, in a dream, the lovely queen saw the god sitting on her lap in the form of a child at play: he whom the hosts of gods worship; dark, four-armed, holding conch, discus, mace and lotus; the Shrivatsa mark on his chest and the Kaustubha jewel at his throat; wearing the Vaijayanti garland and yellow silk; a shining jewelled crown on his head and glittering makara earrings; his cheeks bright with a gentle smile, his face serene; long-eyed as a white lotus, fine-nosed, with auspicious marks; every limb a playground of beauty, wonderful; Kamala at his side; adorned with a garland of wild flowers; the god in whom all gods dwell, decked with every ornament; his lotus feet marked with the thunderbolt, lines, banner and parasol.

8Not long after, the wife of King Shah bore in her womb this mighty form, a portion of the great Vishnu.

9With that great radiance inside her womb she shone like a bank of autumn cloud with the sun hidden within.

10Bearing that radiant child in her womb, the daughter of the lord of the Yadavas, alas, adorned the whole earth.

11Then, longing for the cravings of pregnancy and slow with the weight of the child, she with her broad hips found even her ornaments heavy.

12Her pale face, beyond compare, by its brightness mocked the orb of the autumn moon.

13It is no wonder that the beautiful woman's body grew heavy, since the god, guru of the world, had himself entered her womb.

14Her friends who waited on her thought that pale-faced, pregnant lady quite unlike the goddesses.

15–18Then day after day she had many cravings: to ride horses, elephants and elephant-mounts; to sit firm on a golden throne under a white parasol; to have banners raised and fine chowries waved; to hear the sound of kettledrums; to do heroic deeds; to bear arrows, bows, spears, swords and armour; to win victory; to make great gifts; and to set dharma on its feet.

Skilled old women of good family and character attended her day and night, in the service of the prince; famous attendants followed her wishes and always did what was good for her; wise women with nectar in their hands, trusted ones, nursed the pregnant lady. Childhood

The prince of poets said:

1His parents did not know that the lovely child at play in a child's body was Urukrama, Vishnu, more than human.

2–3Come down to the earth's surface to end the earth's burden, playing in a child's form in the house of King Shah, delighting his mother and the people with his beauty, the Self of all performed there his various deeds.

4–5He who, to do what Indra wished, had again and again slain the sons of Diti in battle after battle with his four arms raising their weapons, and who sleeps, as if tired, in the middle of the ocean of milk — he now cried for his mother's breast.

6Playing as a child and crawling on emerald floors, he would try hard to catch his own reflection.

7As he crawled on his knees, the crystals set in the courtyard took on the colour of rubies from the glow of his lotus-like soles.

8As he crawled about the courtyard of the house, grey with dust, he delighted his mother with the jingle of his jewelled anklets.

9Playing and crawling eagerly about the jewelled courtyard, he vied at every step, alas, with his own reflection.

10He by whose grace all the gods drink nectar — ah, he too ate a soft lump of earth and was delighted.

11The god who, tricking Bali, crossed the three worlds in three strides, now had to struggle, alas, to get over the threshold of the house.

12The lord who is himself the support of the seven worlds stood up holding on to his nurse's finger.

13–14Seeing the sun's disc reflected on the walls of white crystal, he pointed it out with his forefinger, crying "Take it in your hand and give it to me", and, as if beguiled by his own innocence, made his mother laugh.

15–16Seeing him at the door of the house like a young elephant, his head grey with the dust he had thrown up with the lotus of his hand, adorned with the first shoots of teeth like jasmine buds, the nurse who had come to teach him stood amazed.

17Delighted by the nurse clapping her hands, he danced in many ways with a smiling face.

18He who had taught the creator the Vedas with their marks of accent now learned the names of things from his nurse's mouth.

19Pleased at his own wish, he would quickly take off the ornaments on his body, one after another, and give them to his nurses.

20Though master of them all, he set aside his father's elephants and horses, and liked best his elephants and horses of clay.

21Shining with a tuft of hair, he ran after the peacocks to get their tail feathers.

22Imitating the cries of peacocks, parrots and cuckoos, he made people think those very birds were calling.

23Standing beside his loving nurse, this tiger-cub of a child would suddenly roar and give her a fright.

24Untiring, he sometimes whirled round and round like a spinning top; sometimes, delighted, he neighed like a horse.

25Sometimes he trumpeted loudly like an elephant.

26Sometimes, proud of himself, he imitated a kettledrum with deep, sweet sounds that filled heaven and earth.

27Sometimes he had the boys build tall piles of earth and said, "These are my forts."

28Sometimes, playing hide-and-seek, he hid in a corner of the house, and when his friends searched him out and touched him he laughed.

29Sometimes he struck the ball, which he had dropped from his hand and which bounced up again and again, to knock it down.

30–31Sometimes, his black eyes fixed on the ball he had thrown high, as it fell from the sky far above, looking up with his face raised, beating the ground with both feet as if dancing, he caught it in his raised hands.

32He at the sight of whom men are instantly freed from the whirl of birth never felt dizzy from his whirling.

33Though his nurses forbade him and threatened him with a finger, the lion-cub of King Shah kept to all his childish games.

34Told "Eat," he would not eat; told "Drink," he would not drink; coaxed by his nurses with "Sleep," he would not sleep.

35Lost in this game and that, he, the supreme Self of the world, went far off even when his mother called him.

36Wherever Shiva went, absorbed in the delight of his childish play, the gods led by Vasava followed and guarded him.

37The glorious one, the treasure of compassion, the support of the gods, sung in the Vedanta and famed in the Puranas as the Primal Person, who was born, they say, in King Shah's palace to remove the earth's fear — he took on the great beauty of the age called childhood.

The learned men said:

1You have told us, Paramananda, that King Shiva was born on the hill of Shivneri. We have a doubt about this.

2–3For that hill, very famous, valiant and renowned in the world as a second Dharagiri, was the Nizam's and served his interest. How did King Shah come there? Tell us everything, son of Bhatta Govinda.

The prince of poets said:

4Listen, best of the twice-born, to this story of King Shah, sweeter than nectar and holy.

5–8When Ambar, the Abyssinian, bright as the sun, had set, the Nizam, left without a minister, grew doubtful about his own survival. When by fate Ibrahim too, who had been his match, went to heaven, and his son Mahmud took his place; and when the armies of Shahjahan, who had become lord of Delhi, came proudly to the southern quarter to seize it; then the strong-armed King Shah, wishing to help the Nizam because of their old ties, left the city called Vijaya.

9Jadhavrao too left the copper-faced men, whom he had followed, and came over to Dharagiri, taking the Nizam's side.

10–11Meanwhile, Vijayraj, son of Siddhapal, of the line of Vishvasrao, greatly trusted by the Nizam, a devotee of Shiva, of great vows, famous, very wealthy, lived as a king on the hill of Shivneri.

12He thought his daughter Jayanti a fit match for the excellent Shambhuraja, son of King Shah.

13Thinking that alliance most praiseworthy on earth, King Shah too asked for Jayanti as a daughter-in-law.

14Then, as befitted the alliance, a great festival was held between the two of them, Vijayraj and King Shah.

15A great gathering took place of the kings of Vishvasrao's line, of great energy, and of the Bhosles.

16The great wedding of Jayanti and Shambhuraja, a pair with qualities worth praising, was held there at Shivneri.

17–18Then, after some days, when the great festival was over, King Shah, with his kinsman's consent, left his pregnant wife there on top of that very hill with his own people, and set out, wishing to conquer Daryakhan.

19So I have told you how King Shah came to be at Shivneri. What else do you wish to hear?

The learned men said:

20–21When the great King Jadhavrao, of great vows and strong arms, had suddenly left the lord of Delhi and come over to the Nizam's side, and was ready to fight the copper-faced men, who were attacking, what did the Nizam do to get what he wanted?

The prince of poets said:

22Then, alas, as fate would have it, the mind of the Nizam, whose heart was set on pleasures, was turned the wrong way by the wicked ministers who served him.

23To that madman the bad seemed good, and the good, even a man of sweet speech, seemed bad.

24With his perverted sight he made light of his elders, and soon lost his respect even for elders rich in virtue.

25Alas, the great kingdom of that fickle-minded madman, a drinker of wine and a speaker of what should not be spoken, went to ruin.

26Then, when the very splendid lord of the Yadavas came to pay his respects, the evil-minded Nizam insulted him.

27Insulted by the Nizam, the great and proud lord of the Yadavas then flew into a heroic rage.

28–29Then the wicked generals, Hamid and the others, primed beforehand by the Nizam's evil counsel, surrounded the lord of the Yadavas at the edge of the gateway of the hall as he was turning back, proud as a maddened elephant.

30There, fighting many of them, with his sons, ministers and kin, he was welcomed by hosts of gods and gazed on the world of the gods.

31–32Like the toppling of Meru, the fall of the sun, the death of Death himself or the burning of the lord of the waters, the end of the lord of the Yadavas there was, alas, a calamity for all the seven worlds.

33Hearing of that fate of his father-in-law, the lord of the Yadavas, the famous King Shah gave up helping the Nizam.

34Then the army of the copper-faced men quickly crossed the Tapi and laid siege to Dharagiri, held by the Nizam.

35At that moment the very proud and greedy Adil gathered his own army and sent it towards Dharagiri.

36Shahjahan's army and Mahmud's fought each other there day after day, each wanting to take Dharagiri.

37The Nizam Shah himself, standing on the summit of Dharagiri, then fought both those armies.

38Then the Nizam was quickly overwhelmed by the very strong armies of the copper-faced men and by Mahmud's armies, which fought on.

39–40Then, with that fort, his army of many kinds, his foolish minister Fateh Khan, his whole household and his great treasure, he sank there in the ocean of the copper-faced men's army.

The learned men said:

41–45He who had eighty thousand swift horses and eighty-four mountain forts, and many forts on land and in water; whose rich country was under his sway, unassailable by enemies; who at every step took alive the whole army of the proud Adil Shah and of the lord of Delhi; at whose sudden attack, as at the swoop of a falcon, enemy birds hid in terror — for what reason did that Nizam then perish? Tell us, prince of poets, for we wish to hear.

The prince of poets said:

46–47When his father Ambar, who cared for all, had died, Fateh Khan, a man of little sense, by the force of what was to be, became the Nizam's minister and, valiant and pitiless as Death, tormented the people.

48–49From the day the Nizam, on the counsel of the evil-minded Hamid, had the king of the Yadavas killed, all the kings, King Shah and the rest, and the Muslim soldiers too, grew sick at heart.

50–51Distrustful, angry and frightened, some took refuge with Adil, some with the lord of Delhi; some cruel-hearted men turned to open hostility; and some, alas, made a show of standing aside.

52Through the wickedness of that evil-minded man, terrible signs then appeared to bring about the destruction of living beings.

53For a long time the rain did not fall on his country; grain became very hard to get, while gold became cheap.

54Rich men gave jewels in exchange for a mere measure of grain and with great trouble obtained a few measures of horse-gram.

55With nothing to eat, crying out in despair, beasts ate beasts and men, robbed of their senses, ate men.

56–57Through that great drought, the attack of the enemy, and the lack of the old ministers and of a large army, the evil-minded Fateh Khan and the lord of Dharagiri, wasting away moment by moment, were caught by the copper-faced men, who followed in their footsteps.

58When time is favourable, everything favours a man; when it turns against him, everything is against him.

59For the embodied man to whom the blessed, eternal Time is favourable, all tasks succeed without effort.

60Birth, existence, growth, ripening, decline and destruction: these six states are changes made by Time.

61–62Victory and defeat, enmity, the strength or weakness of counsel, learning and ignorance, generosity and meanness, activity and rest, freedom and dependence, prosperity and adversity — all arise as Time turns.

63Birth, life and death, those three states, come from Time, and so do sacrifices and other rites.

64–70Without Time there is no seed, without Time no sprout; without Time no flower, without Time no fruit; without Time no holy place, without Time no austerity; without Time no success, without Time no victory. Without Time the moon, fire and sun do not shine; without Time the ocean does not rise and fall. Without Time Bhagiratha did not bring down the Ganga; without Time King Nriga was not freed from being a lizard; without Time Rama did not kill the ten-headed one; without Time Vibhishana did not gain Lanka; without Time Krishna did not hold up Govardhana; without Time Partha did not kill Vaikartana. Time alone is the cause of joys and sorrows. I believe Time is the Lord, maker of creation, preservation and destruction.

71–72Broken in battle, the Nizam perished; the demon lord of Delhi gained Devagiri and rejoiced; and Adil Shah too was put to shame by the rout of his army. Know, Brahmins, that all of this came about through Time.

73When the copper-faced men had blockaded Dharagiri, a fort hard to take, and seized the Nizam there, Adil's army, its pride gone, withdrew, they say.

Thus ends the eighth canto in the Anupurana composed by Kavindra Paramananda.

Shivaji learns to write; Bangalore

The prince of poets said:

1–2Then, when the lord of Delhi, having gained Devagiri, was glad at heart, and the mad Mahmud was dejected because his army had been routed, King Shah quickly brought under his control by force many of the Nizam Shah's mountain forts, Shivneri first among them.

3–4Overrunning step by step the whole country lying along the most holy Godavari, the mighty Pravara, the Nira with water like the ocean of milk, and the fearsome Bhimarathi, he quickly brought the Sahya mountains too under his control.

5–7When he stood against the lord of Delhi, as against Indra, the Maharashtra kings came over to this great king: the Ghatges, the Kantes, the Gaikwads, the Kanks, the Dhomares, the Chavans, the Mohites, the Mahadiks, the Kharates, the Pandhares, the Waghs, the Ghorpades and others. King Shah made them captains of his army.

8Then the son of Jahangir, wishing to conquer Shah, at once made a treaty with Adil Shah.

9Then, as those two sought to conquer King Shah, the king of kings, in war, the great river named Bhima became the boundary between them.

The learned men said:

10–11For how many years did the bold King Shah, valiant as a lion, fight with the armies of Shahjahan and of Adil? And how did the lord make peace with both of them? Prince of poets, we wish to hear this from you.

The prince of poets said:

12Shah, of a thousand-rayed sun's valour, fought the armies of Shahjahan and Adil for three years.

13Then the matted-haired god, lord of dreams, came to him in a dream, and as Shah bowed to him he spoke, lighting up the quarters with the gleam of his teeth:

The matted-haired god said:

14"This lord of Delhi, of great splendour, is very hard to conquer in the world. So stop this unending fighting, wise one.

15"Because of the fierce austerity this wicked man once performed, he will not be destroyed until it is exhausted.

16"All these Muslims are of the line of the elder gods, the demons, and at every step they hate the gods and the gods on earth, the Brahmins.

17"The one who has come down to earth to kill these Muslims is the blessed Vishnu himself, your child named Shiva.

18"He will soon do what you have in mind. So wait a while, strong-armed one."

19When the god with matted locks, pleased, had spoken thus, the lord of the earth woke at dawn with a clear mind.

20Then Shah gave the Nizam's country, except his own territory, partly to the lord of Delhi and partly to Adil.

21Though stubborn by nature, Shah, at the command of the three-eyed god, gave up his stubbornness and made peace with Adil and the copper-faced men.

22–25Then, when the copper-faced men, having gained the Nizam's country and being glad at heart, had withdrawn to their own lands after their show of strength, the very wise Adil, thinking himself weak, reflected in his heart: "These copper-faced men, who sank the Nizam, a breaker of enemy armies, in battle, will probably sink me too. So I shall place this strong-armed king, son of Maloji, in my service, and do what I wish.

26–28"Formerly my father Ibrahim, relying on his strength, destroyed his enemies and was free of fear. But after he went to heaven I foolishly slighted him, and he left me and went away. This great and proud king, strong-armed, was honoured by Ibrahim more than by me and raised with affection."

29Thinking this over in his mind, the very splendid Mahmud at once sent ministers to King Shah.

30Persuaded there by those ministers, skilled in counsel, the strong-armed one promised to help Adil.

31Then Mahmud, having gained King Shah's support, had great joy at every step.

32–33Then the valiant Mahmud sent Randulla, son of Farad Khan, a man able to bear the burden of war, dear to the whole army and foremost of generals, together with the strong great King Shah, to conquer the Karnataka region.

34–35Then Randulla set out with Farad Khan, Yakut Khan, Ankush Khan, Husain, Ambar Khan and Masud, and with fine warriors — the Pawars, Ghatges, Ingles, Gades, Ghorpades and others.

36The great-minded, strong King Bhosle went with that general to the Karnataka country.

37–42The valiant Shah brought under his control Virabhadra, the very powerful lord of Bindupura; the famous Kenga Nayaka, ruler of Vrishapattana; Jagadeva, strong-armed lord of Kaveripattana; the cruel Kanthirava, lord of Shrirangapattana; the hero Vijayaraghava, lord of Tanjapura; the bold Venkata Nayaka of Tanji; the proud Trimalla Nayaka of Madhura; the arrogant Venkata, ruler of the Pilugunda region; the steady Shriranga Raja, lord of Vidyanagara; the famous Tammagauda, lord of Hamsakuta; and other kings besides. He pleased the general Randulla.

43–44Then, after a war that slaughtered heroes by night and day, the victorious king lived in the city called Bengrul, very lovely, which had been taken from the warlike Kempagauda and given to him by Randulla as a reward.

45–55That excellent king, equal to Vasava, lived happily in many ways with his own people in that fine city: with its strong walls and gateways; its white plastered mansions whose banners scraped the sky; its interiors filled with the lovely houses of craftsmen and artists; its roofs and ledges cooing with dove-pairs perched on them; adorned with the cries of peacocks flying up from the windows; its broad bazaars stocked with goods for sale; with wells of glittering water at every step and long step-wells in bloom; its lakes bright with many lilies; its grounds made fine with clusters of flowering trees; with walls on which bright pictures delighted the eye; with lovely rows of houses of many colours joined together; adorned with gates, market-squares, roofs and floors of opened stone; guarded on all sides by expert armies; shining with a moat full of water reaching the underworld; fenced with a rampart like a boundless ocean; with sweet bowers waving in the breeze; and filled with temples whose golden finials shone. Sometimes he went hunting, sometimes he waited on holy men, sometimes he worshipped Shambhu, and sometimes he discussed poetry.

56–59Sometimes he took pleasure in watching dancing girls dance; sometimes he practised drawing the bow in many ways; sometimes he inspected the weapons laid out in the armoury; sometimes he reviewed the troops he had gathered; sometimes he made excursions to the city gardens, fragrant with flowers; sometimes he enjoyed the pleasures of love with doe-eyed women; sometimes he practised yogic postures according to the art taught in the shastras of yoga. So the lord passed his time, each pleasure in its season.

60There, though the inner apartments held many lovely women, the mother of Shambhu and Shiva, the daughter of the Yadava, held her husband's heart.

61As Vasudeva shone with Halin and Hari, so Shah shone with Shambhu and Shiva.

62The great king loved his son Shiva greatly, younger than Shambhu but greater in the wealth of his virtues.

63From the day that son Shiva was born, all Shah's riches grew.

64Many elephants stood at his door, lovely as Mount Mandara, their temples outshining the lotus, pouring out the water of rut.

65In his stables were thousands of lovely Sindhi horses, swift as the wind, unstoppable in battle.

66His treasury grew daily, and with it his anger; his valour and power increased day by day.

67Forts hard to take became easy for him; he was always victorious; not even in a dream did he know defeat.

68Flowers, fruits and crops increased, and all the successes he wished for came to him even without effort.

69Raised so high by that son, the soul of the Primal Person, the son of Malaji rejoiced greatly.

70Then, seeing that his son, endowed with virtues, was seven years old, the king thought: "He is fit to learn his letters."

71He seated his son, intelligent and clear of speech, on his teacher's lap together with the ministers' sons of his own age.

72Before the teacher had finished telling him to write the first letter, he had written the second as well and showed it.

73The teacher made him learn the whole of the script, which is the doorway to all learning.

74The teacher, his mind gratified, seeing that lion-cub of a king — naturally intelligent, of good temper, of a power beyond words — learn the script so fast, felt in the gathering of the learned that this was something quite out of the ordinary.

Thus ends the ninth canto in the Samhita, the Anupurana brought to light by Paramananda of Nivasa.

Shivaji comes to Pune; his education

The prince of poets said:

1–2When Shiva reached his twelfth year, King Shah, at the command of the bearer of Pinaka, called the younger brother of Shambhu, Shiva, bright as the sun and of good marks, and made the far-seeing one lord of the Pune country.

The learned men said:

3–4Prince of poets Paramananda, tell us how, at the command of the blessed god, the enemy of Tripura, the king sent Shiva to the Pune country, and how he reached that country at his father's order.

The prince of poets said:

5–10Once, after worshipping the blessed god whose banner is the bull in his palace, the virtuous King Shah lay on a comfortable bed. In a dream he saw before him, smiling, the enemy of Tripura: his five faces serene, with ten arms and three eyes; lovely with matted locks moist with the waters of the Mandakini; the crescent of the cool-rayed moon on his head, his brow lovely with the triple mark; his throat like an emerald; adorned with serpents; giver of boons and of fearlessness; a hero bearing many weapons; wearing a leopard skin as upper garment and an elephant skin as lower; the source of every liberation, the treasure-house of every fortune; a yogi, lord of yogis, worshipped by Indra, Upendra and the rest; with all the worlds, and with the mountain's daughter.

11Seeing the Lord himself before him, he bowed, and stood before him with folded hands, deeply content.

12Then the blessed Bharga, whose power none can check, showed his favour to his devotee, the lord of the earth, with these words:

The Lord said:

13"Strong-armed King Shah of the line of the Sun, great of mind, listen to my words, which are for your good.

14"Know that this younger son of yours, who shines beside you, marked with good signs, is the Supreme Person.

15"Your son Urukrama, growing step by step, will overrun the whole earth and kill the Muslims.

16"This blessed goddess Girija, who loves her devotees, will come to him from time to time and protect him.

17"Remover of the earth's burden, slayer of hostile kings, this devotee of mine will be unconquerable by everyone.

18"So appoint this strong-armed, great-souled one named Shiva to the honoured lordship of the Pune country."

19Having said this, the Lord himself put a string of pearls around the prince's neck.

20When the god with matted locks had shown himself in this way, his heart full of love, the lord of the earth woke at the hour of Brahma.

21–22Marvelling, his mind astonished at his own austerity, meditating again and again on that very form of the Lord, in the morning he called his family priest Prabhakara, bright as the sun, and told him his dream.

23Then, with the approval of that delighted priest, Shah appointed Shiva to the lordship of the Pune country.

24Given that lordship by his valiant father and eager to go to his own territory, King Shiva shone.

25–27Then, on an auspicious day, King Shah sent his virtuous son to the Pune country with some elephants, horses and foot soldiers; with hereditary and trusted ministers and famous teachers; with titles, tall banners and a large treasury; and with attendants of unequalled deeds.

28Then, in a few days, the scion of the Sun went from the Karnataka country to the land of Maharashtra.

29With the three powers — of might, of energy and of counsel — and with his gathered armies and his own fortune, Shiva reached the holy city of Pune.

30Friend of the chakra, who at once made the whole land glad, the people saw that friend of the world but did not know him for what he was.

The verse plays on the sun, friend of the chakravaka bird and of the world: Shiva, friend of the realm, came like the sun.

31Then, with ministers in harmony with him and delighting his subjects, the valiant one grew step by step, and his fame grew with him.

32Through his support, the country of Maharashtra became rich and full of people, and lived up to its name, "the great kingdom".

33The teachers, humble and virtuous, who had approached him with devotion and with whom he was one in mind, found their purpose fulfilled.

34–40He became expert in the Shruti, Smriti and Puranas, the Bharata and the science of government, in all the shastras, poetry and the Ramayana, in physical exercise, architecture, horoscopy and mathematics, in the science of the bow and of medicine, in physiognomy, in the various languages, metres and fine sayings; in riding elephants, horses and chariots and in their marks; in mounting, swimming, running and leaping; in the sword, bow, discus, lance, broadsword and spear; in battle and wrestling; in making forts impregnable and in storming them; in hitting targets hard to see; in reading gestures, in illusions, in drawing out poison and the like, in judging gems, in concentration, and in scripts. Thus the all-pervading one, who already knew them all, by mastering them himself, joined each of those teachers to great fame.

41As the wish-granting tree of the gods shines in the splendour of spring, so he shone at the start of youth with fresh beauty.

42–43To him, past boyhood and in the bloom of new youth, lovely with a beauty like the love-god's, came a wife, a virtuous and well-conducted woman of lovely form, shining with every virtue, born of the Pawar family.

44Winning this fair-skinned woman, his love in a former birth, he rejoiced as Shri Krishna did in winning Rukmini.

45Then, as that couple, familiar to each other from a former birth, came to love each other, the three aims of life — dharma, wealth and love — met in them and took on great splendour.

Thus ends the tenth canto in the Samhita, the Anupurana of the line of the Sun, brought to light by Kavindra Paramananda of Nidhivasa.

Shahaji in Karnataka; Mustafa Khan arrives

The learned men said:

1After sending his son Shiva, with his counsellors, to the Pune country, what did King Shah do while living in the Karnataka country?

2And how did Mahmud himself treat Shah, who had conquered his enemies and whose zeal in war was famous?

The prince of poets said:

3By the use of the six measures of policy and by the strength of his various counsels, Shah brought the whole Karnataka country under his control.

4Jagadeva, seeking to bow in friendship, received his command on his head as if it were a flower.

5Even the invincible lord of Madhura became obedient to him; the lord of Mahishur too submitted to his authority.

6Virabhadra, whose fine throne had been seized by force by the wicked Randulla Khan, sat on it again under his protection.

7Using one counsel or another as occasion required, that wise man by his power removed the Muslims' fear of many people.

8Through King Shah's counsel, Randulla, very hard for others to bear, carried out all his master's business.

9–10Then, when the general Randulla had gone the way of Time, whatever general Adil sent there to bring the kings of Karnataka quickly under his control followed Shah's lead, wishing for success.

11Then the son of Ibrahim, out of pride and choosing the wrong course, ordered Mustafa Khan to seize King Bhosle.

12–15Then, outroaring the ocean with the thunder of his drums, filling the quarters with the cries of victory of his warriors, scaring the clouds with his fluttering banners, frightening the clouds with the raised trunks of his elephants, blotting out the sun with the dust raised by the hooves of his horses, drying up rivers with his flowing streams of troops, levelling the earth as if its heights and hollows were made one, he reached the Karnataka country, held by enemy warriors.

19As they showed each other more and more affection, a great festival took place on the road at their meeting, like a meeting of friends.

20There the two gave each other clothes, ornaments, elephants and horses in abundance.

21Then the strong King Bhosle encamped his army near the camp of Mustafa Khan's army.

22Whenever Mustafa Khan, seeking a weak point, looked at King Shah, he found him fully on guard.

23To win his trust, Mustafa made a show of friendship and put Shah first in every piece of business.

24–28Rising quickly to greet him, going far out to meet him, gladly embracing him and clasping his hands, giving him half his seat, turning his face to him, speaking with a smile, looking at him with eyes eager for affection, revealing the plans of his various schemes, giving him great precedence in every matter, honouring him with gifts and presents, praising and flattering him, joking with him with much humour, telling him of spiritual matters, rousing his sense of what was good for him, and telling him of his own affairs — in these ways the Muslim showed him trust day after day.

29Then that guardian of the army, skilled in policy, called together all the captains of his army and spoke these words in private:

Mustafa Khan said:

30"When a servant lives on a man and eats his food, his life belongs to that man and is never his own.

31"Without learning there is no greatness; without genius no poetry; and without a master's favour no wish is ever seen fulfilled.

32"So those who know policy call that man blessed who, for his master's sake, gives up his own life.

33"To those devoted to serving their master, no kinsman, friend, relative or brother born of the same womb — not even a father — should come first.

34"What man, if he is pleased when his master is pleased and ruined when his master is angry, would not serve him with an undivided mind?

35"So let all of us now, standing firm in this great rule, strive together for Mahmud's good.

36–37"My proud master Mahmud has sent me the message: 'Shah must be seized.' So, as long as the strong King Bhosle remains unaware, we who want our own good must do it.

38"When this night has passed, at daybreak, all of you together with your own soldiers, seize the king."

39Told this by Mustafa for the success of his aim, the captains of the army went each to his own camp.

40On the very night they made this plan, great portents appeared in Shah's camp.

41Horses wept, elephants cried out piteously, and banners broke without warning with a harsh crack.

42Drums sounded harshly though no one beat them, and whirlwinds spread dust suddenly.

43Hail fell all around without clouds, and lightning flashed in a cloudless sky.

44At lamp-lighting time the lamps would not light, and men's faces and minds were not glad.

45Near the army jackals made inauspicious cries, and dogs, raising their faces, howled most dismally.

46Owls came very close again and again and hooted, and packs of wolves suddenly made cruel cries.

47In every temple the images of the gods trembled, and at midnight the cows suddenly lowed.

48Many such signs foretelling fear arose, yet the king, in the power of fate, did not become wary.

49At midnight spies came and reported: "The commanders are standing ready in their own camps, and Mustafa has held long counsel with the chiefs of the army in the middle of the night." Yet, though he heard it, the very strong King Shah, being subject to fate, did not do what the moment required.

Shahaji's arrest

1–11Then, when the sun, friend of the lotus, ending the grief of the chakravaka parted from its mate, bright as a bandhuka flower, was about to climb the peak of the eastern mountain; when the courtyard of the sky was reddened by the glow of dawn; when the quarters had been freed from the night-prowler darkness; when the breeze blew, cooled by spray, gently stirring the Pampa lake and sweet with the scent of blooming lotuses — then, at the order of Mustafa, these men surrounded King Shah's camp: Dilavar, Masud, Sarja Yakut, Ambar; the lord of Adavani and the lord of Karnapura; Farad and the two brothers Khairat; Yakut, Atash, Azam, Bahlol, the proud Malik Rahan; Raghav and Mamba; Vedoji, son of Bhaskar; the very strong Ballal, son of King Haibat; three Brahmin brothers mad with pride, of the army; the Pawars Siddha and Mamba, the strong Bhosle; and other army captains born of various families. Maddened by the lust of battle, bearing great weapons, they seemed to shake the sky with their mass of banners, to pound the earth with the tips of their prancing horses' hooves, to burn up the whole three worlds with their masses of energy, to build a wall all around with their strong forces, blazing like fire and fearless as Death.

12–13Its elephants unsaddled, its horses unharnessed, its soldiers not ready, its commanders asleep, its sentries drowsy from the night's watch — that camp of King Shah was thrown into confusion by many fears.

14As the sun's disc is ringed by a great halo, so Shah's camp was ringed by the enemy army.

15Mustafa himself, lord of the troops, guarded the rear of that army roaring like the ocean on every side.

16–20Then, with his kinsmen Khandoji, Ambaji and Manaji around him, and surrounded by men bearing swords, bows, lances, javelins and discs, the strong-armed Baji Raje Ghorpade, of terrible deeds; Jasvant Wadve, another submarine fire; Maloji, ornament of the Pawar family; and the famous King Tuloji of the Bhosle line — all these strongest of the strong, pounding the earth with the hooves of the horses they rode, entered Shah's camp.

21Roaring loudly, eager to conquer and drunk with pride, they woke the sleeping Shah, as elephants might wake a lion.

22As soon as he woke and heard that the enemy had come, Shah, while arming himself, ordered his soldiers loudly: "Get ready!"

23Then a cry of "Horse, horse! Sword, sword! Lance, lance!" broke out in the camp of Shah's men.

24–25Then, while the strong-armed and mighty Shah was arming, and his great army was gripped by sudden fear, Khandoji Patil, like a lion whose course is never checked in battle after battle, rode alone against the Ghorpades.

26–27Wearing armour, carrying a glorious shield of rhinoceros hide, sure of hand, lance in hand, sword at his side, knowing the work of war — when he rode quickly onto the field, Baji Raje and the others then gave lion-roars.

28Then the Ghorpade captains, roused to fury, surrounded that Patil, the crest-jewel of his family, with anger.

29Then the radiant man whirled his lance, which licked the clouds, and made his horse turn thousands of circles.

30In the space around that whirling lance, a circle of light shone like the disc of the sun.

31With that lance he furiously cut to pieces the great warriors who struck at him there with their various weapons.

32As mountains fell when the angry wielder of the thunderbolt struck them, so many men fell to the ground, every limb broken.

33Though struck hard by other warriors biting their lips in anger, he showed his manhood.

34Then with his long, sharp lance he cut off someone's head, like a mountain peak.

35In the same way, in his fury, that best of the strong pierced someone's chest, broad as a slab of rock and impenetrable.

36With a blow of the blade of his lance he at once struck down a horseman's shoulder, high as an elephant's temple.

37Khandoji cut off one man's feet, another's waist, another's throat.

38Leaping up again and again and striking down enemies all around with his lance, the Patil, though alone, seemed there to be many.

39Wounded by hundreds of sharp weapons, his armour broken by the host of attacking enemies, he drove back the elephant-like foes.

40His body cut by the arrows his enemies shot, his limbs wet with blood, he shone like the red planet Mars.

41Seeing the wonderful skill of the Patil as he fought, Baji Raje and the other warriors roared and slapped their arms.

42In the battle Manaji, breaker of pride, cut his lance in two; and he took a cruel sword in his hand.

43Then, seeing him sword in hand and sure of hand, the strong-armed Baji Raje smiled and challenged him to a great duel.

44Then the Patil struck at Baji Raje's chest with a sword like Death's rod.

45–47When the strong-armed Baji Raje, his broad chest struck by that great blow, fell into a swoon, Ambaji hurled a terrible iron club at Khandoji, Manaji a mace, Mallaji a sharp spear like a tongue of flame, Balaji a matchless lance, Jasvant an arrow, and Khandoji a sword.

48–49All the weapons of the warriors fell on that terrible man from every side; with his own sword he cut some in two, some in three, some in five, some in nine and some in ten.

50Baji Raje, after experiencing for a moment a swoon and for a moment a state beyond all description, fought the Patil again.

51Then with his skill Baji Raje brought down on the Patil a huge mace that struck fear into his enemies.

52–56When, struck by that cruel mace of Baji Raje as the Krauncha mountain was by the spear of Karttikeya, the fine warrior Khandoji Patil had fallen and ridden off in the chariot of the gods, and his own army was in turmoil, being slaughtered by the enemy army — then the strong-armed great King Shah, with his helmet tied on, his turban fluttering, carrying a shield, in armour, tall, best of bowmen, a lance-fighter who had mastered the art of wheeling in circles, mounting a great horse like a cloud riding on a cloud, suddenly attacked Baji Raje, wanting to kill him.

57–59The king of the Abhiras, Dasaji, conqueror of the ten quarters; Yogaji Bhandakar, bearing bow and arrows; Santaji Gunjavatkar, Meghaji Thakur, his brother Tryambakraj, and the proud Dattaraj — these and other captains of the army, and many guards of the rear, protected King Shah on every side by the hundred.

60–62King Baji Raje and the others saw him: filling the sky with the twang of his drawn bow, a hero with the neck of a lion and the shoulders of a bull, of great vigour, guarded by his two brothers Datta and Tryambak, surrounded by his soldiers as Shatakratu by the gods, and suddenly held back by a contrary wind.

63Seeing the enemies with their raised weapons, King Shah roared like a lion, making earth and sky resound.

64The quarters were filled with Shah's roar, and the ocean, at once echoing it, was stirred.

65Baji Raje could not bear Shah's roar, as a maddened elephant cannot bear the trumpeting of another maddened elephant.

66–67Then the Ghorpades, roaring with a terrible thunderous noise, came up calling out their own names — men of great power, in fine armour, skilled in action — and surrounded Shah as clouds surround the moon.

68–69Then the two kings Tryambak and Datta, Dasaji, the strong-armed Meghaji, bright as Mahasena, Yogaji, the Gunjavatkar and others, wishing to protect King Shah, attacked the enemy warriors.

70–72Then Manaji fought Tryambakraj, bow in hand; Khandoji, conqueror of the wicked, fought Dattaraj; Mallaji, conqueror of rivals, fought Yogaji; Ambuji fought Meghaji, who had a voice like thunder; Baji Raje fought the valiant King Shah; and many others fought many other long-armed men.

73Twanging their bows, eager to conquer one another, maddened with pride, the great warriors danced like actors on the stage of battle.

74Foot soldiers fought foot soldiers there, horsemen horsemen, and many elephant riders elephant riders, each wanting to conquer.

75–77Spearmen struck spearmen, those of firm grip those of firm grip, club-men with terrible clubs, mallet-men with mallets, swordsmen with swords, javelin-men with sharp javelins, mace-men with maces, men with discs by the thousand with discs, archers with sharp arrows, dagger-men with daggers, lancers with lances: so they then struck one another in great numbers.

78–80Heads with their helmets, chests with their armour, hands with their gauntlets, arms with their armlets, banners with their staffs, bows with their arrows, horses with their riders, elephants with their drivers — these, cut down by the weapons hurled by the enemy, fell to the ground then by the many hundreds on both sides.

81Headless trunks ran about here and there, drawing bows, with guards on their fingers.

82Alas! Whoever had cut off a man's head or any limb, the trunk ran at him in that very instant and attacked him.

83–90Then, as pools of blood formed from the bodies of horses, elephants and men cut apart by sharp arrows; as the ground grew slippery with marrow, flesh, fat and lard; as the hosts of dakinis rejoiced with dancing goblins; as the circle of Bhairavis, with ear-rings made from the skulls of this and that captain, grew wild with drink; as the moon-crested god rejoiced with his goblins, made glad by the lovely garlands of the heads of heroes who had pierced the disc of the sun — Datta Raje, alas, had his hand cut off there by King Khandoji; King Tryambak, alas, was beaten by King Manaji; Meghaji, struck by the blows of weapons hurled by Ambuji, was terrified; Dasaji too fled; his own army was failing and confused, like an ocean being drunk by the sage Agastya; and the Bhandakar was harassed by Malaji with showers of arrows. Then King Shah covered King Baji with sharp arrows.

91Though his body was torn by those very sharp arrows, Baji Raje, of great energy, did not faint.

92Then, seeing King Shah there with his glittering armour, he struck him on the arm with his lance.

93Skilled in the science of war, his body hard as a thunderbolt, King Shah was not troubled by that lance blow.

94–95Then the valiant great King Shah was surrounded by fighters armed with arrows, spears, maces, swords, lances and other weapons — Manaji and the others, their anger rising, guarding Baji Raje and showing their own valour.

96There the valiant lord of the earth scorched them all with arrows whose touch was like the flame of the fire at the end of time.

97–98Then hundreds of soldiers, cut to pieces by Shah's arrows, their arms broken off, red with flowing blood, fell before the eyes of Baji Raje, like flowering palasha trees struck down at once by a fierce gale.

99Wherever he rode, mounted on his leaping horse, there thousands of his enemies fled in terror.

100Struck down by blows like thunderbolts, they were shattered like mountains into ten, a hundred, a thousand pieces.

101–102Then one man cut down Shah's attendant, another his companion, one his matchless armour, another his shield with its crescent boss; then one Ghorpade cut his quiver and his bow, and another his tall banner.

103–106Then, when the great and strong-armed king — showered by the rain of weapons hurled by the enemy, quickly leaping from his horse, moving like the enemy of serpents, his limbs red with the blood flowing from his body pierced by arrows, bewildered by the strain of the great battle — embraced the ground; and when the enemy host was roaring and slapping its arms; then a cry of grief rose loudly in the Bhosles' army.

107–109There, as the steady, righteous King Shah, glorious as Dharmaraja — the very treasure, as it were, of all the world and the refuge of all — lay sunk on the ground in the great waves of a swoon, like the sun fallen from the sky by fate, the strong Balaji quickly leapt from his horse and, showing himself a kinsman, protected him with his own shield. Then King Baji himself took the strong-armed Shah,

110as a snake-charmer seizes a hissing serpent; and then the enemies set him on an elephant and led him away.

111–112Seeing him fainting, his mouth dry, his eyes closed, like a lion cast into a cage, King Shah, deceived by the evil-minded Mustafa, a master of deceit, the people lamented loudly.

113We think that Shah, though he knew everything, did not see the deceit with which the Muslim covered him as with a cloth.

114Ah! Mustafa, wanting to do his master's business, to win his trust swore by his own son and broke his oath on his own Book.

115So the people everywhere then condemned that Muslim; but he, who wanted only his master's business done, thought he had done well.

116–118Not a saddle, not a horse, not an elephant, not a camel; not a weapon nor a man to bear it; not an instrument nor a player; not a couch, not a canopy, not a flag, not a banner; nothing to sell, no seller, no fuel, no peg; no tent-cloth, not even a pavilion was left there: such, in half a moment, did Shah's camp become.

119Mahmud rejoiced greatly when he heard from his messengers that Mustafa and the others, like the fire at the end of the world, had captured in battle the proud Shah, as the wielder of the thunderbolt's foes are taken by the guardians of the quarters, and were bringing him under guard.

Thus ends the twelfth canto in the Samhita, the Anupurana brought to light by Kavindra Paramananda.

The battle at Shirwal; Balaji Haibatrao killed

The learned men said:

1Hearing that his father had been captured by the enemy, what did King Shambhu do, and what did King Shiva do?

2And after capturing the great king, what did Mustafa, the commander of the army, do, and the evil-minded, unrighteous Mahmud?

The prince of poets said:

3Shambhu, living in the city of Bengrul, was furious with Mustafa when he heard that his father had been captured by the enemy.

4The valiant King Shiva too, hearing of this plight of King Shah, vowed to pay Adil back for the wrong.

5–6The very proud Mustafa, wanting to take Bengrul, quickly ordered King Tanaji, leader of the Duryas, Vitthal Gopal, a Brahmin living by the warrior's trade, and the bold Farad Khan to set out at once.

7Then the shrewd Mahmud too ordered captains of his army to go to Shiva's country.

8–12Then the general named Fateh Khan, a great-minded man; Mina and Ratan, the two Shaikhs; the wrathful Fateh Khan; the cruel Sharfa Shah, a bowman rich in fame — these Muslims, armoured and equipped with weapons; Ghantika Mattaraj, his arrows like thunderbolts; the strong Baji Nayak, lord of Phaltan; and hundreds of other vassals, with gold-backed bows, golden armour, golden clothes and golden banners, carrying shields that shone with golden moons and bosses — these quickly attacked and took by force the town of Bilvasara.

13–14Likewise the son of King Haibat, named Ballal, sure-handed, skilled and cruel in deeds, like a second Ashvatthama, surrounded by many troops tight-packed like lions, reached the town of Shirval without being checked by Shiva's armies.

15–16Then, hearing of their coming, Shiva, staying on the summit of Purandar and like Karttikeya, son of the slayer of Pura, smiling, armoured, bow and arrows in hand, fully equipped, spoke these words to his steady warriors, equal to the plough-bearer:

Shiva said:

17"My father the great king, rich in his own riches, trusted Mustafa Khan, and alas, he has met with misfortune.

18"Do not trust the untrustworthy, and do not even trust the trusted, for fear born of trust cuts away even the roots.

19"Though he had heard this saying of Vyasa, alas, the lord of the earth put his trust in that man, who was most untrustworthy.

20"The vilest of Muslims, Mustafa, whose intentions and movements cannot be read, at the command of Adil Shah captured the great king.

21"Even King Rama, son of Dasharatha, urged on by his doe-eyed wife, trusted the golden deer and was deceived by the ten-necked one.

22"Yayati, son of Nahusha, too trusting, was deceived by Indra and quickly fell from heaven.

23"Karna, who wore the armour he was born with, trusted the enemy of Bala, was stripped of it, and so was killed by Partha.

24"Even Dharmaraja, foremost of the Bharatas, trusting, was beaten at dice by Shakuni, the helper of Suyodhana.

25"So a discerning man, one who has properly studied the duties of kings with their marks, should never trust enemies.

26"Embracing a deadly serpent, drinking halahala poison, and trusting enemies are reckoned the same.

27"Fondling the young of monkeys, handling a black cobra, and keeping the friendship of the wicked lead equally to harm.

28–30"As lighting a lamp on the porch of a blind man's house, building a dam of sand in a stream, threading broken pearls back together, splitting the stem of a plantain, as the world says, digging the sky or beating the water lead only to toil, so does serving the wicked.

31"What did he do, who did not remember the science of policy, that teaches not to trust an enemy? He made himself a bed of burning khadira coals.

32"If the thirst of the thirsty could be quenched by a mirage, then all good fortune could come from serving the wicked.

33"The wicked man who looks for weak spots and vies with others should be known as more harmful than a snake; he should not be ignored.

34"My great king guarded the whole kingdom of this Mahmud and kept his word. What has he done to him in return?

35"He has become the enemy of those who did him good. Though his fortune makes him honoured, it is a wonder that this Adil Shah, now without support, does not fall.

36"My elder brother, who stays in the city of Bengrul and is besieged there by Farad Khan and other enemies, will fight there.

37"And I, guarding these mountain forts without fail, will fight here with the enemy armies, fully prepared.

38"I here myself, and the valiant King Shambhu there — both of us fighting, we shall set our father free.

39"Then, when we have humbled him, the very arrogant Mahmud will release the great king, and his own pride with him.

40"If Mahmud does not free our honoured father, together with his own dharma, he will reap the fruit of his own deed.

41"If in his folly Adil harms the great king, my father will at once destroy him together with his helpers.

42"Prison and other bonds are not enough to bind those whose minds are always bound by the cords of dharma.

43"Long ago I took the famous Jayavalli and set up there Chandraraj, who coveted it.

44"The fearsome Ghorpades, like angry serpents, have fallen quite quiet on seeing me, the snake-charmer.

45"Before, I set out at once to fight, put the lord of Phaltan to flight, captured him alive and let him go.

46"Now these warriors, Fateh Khan and the rest, gathered together, will meet us and fight like maddened elephants.

47"This strong Ballal, guarding a strong force, has taken Shirval and thinks himself very great.

48"So go there quickly from here, capture that very strong man, and free Shirval today.

49"Then tomorrow, or the day after, here or there, we shall see the mighty Fateh Khan and fight him."

The prince of poets said:

50Hearing Shiva's words, those soldiers by the thousand filled the sky with mighty lion-roars.

51–56Then Godaji, crusher of enemies, born of the Jagtap family, a great fighter who delighted in battle; Bhimaji of the Wagh line, terrible as a second Bhima; Kante, who broke the pride of enemies and the pride of the arrogant; Shivaji, born of the Ingale family, the very ornament of battle, the tip of his lance terrible, like Death; Bhikaji, born of the Chor line, who stole the fortune of enemy heroes, the best of the band, fearless, with a very terrible army, in the work of war; and Bhairav his brother, terrible as Bhairava in battle, who struck terror into enemy hearts — these brave men of the lord of the mountain fort, bright as the sun, splendid each in his own glory, bowed to him and set out.

57Over all of them Shiva appointed Kavaji as commander, as Vasudeva appointed Satyaki over all the Vrishni heroes.

58–59Armoured, roaring like clouds, riding horses in full harness, they came down from Purandar, passed the night there, and in the morning, eager to conquer the enemy, turned towards the march and had the drums beaten.

60–61Then, as if splitting the surface of the earth with the feet of their foot soldiers and as if cutting the circle of the sky with their whirling horses, as if scattering the fire of the world's end upon their enemies, those heroes quickly saw Shirval.

62But seeing Shiva's strong army approach, the enemy said to his own very wise foot soldiers:

Ballal said:

63"Do not be afraid at the sight of this very proud enemy army. In battle, death is best; flight is the worst.

64"At Fateh Khan's command we have come to Shirval. Our position here is fixed, like the fixed star.

65"If you are afraid, then at my command stay here and rely on me as on a second rampart.

66"Our master's business will not be accomplished by our dying alone. So for the great battle let us take refuge in the rampart."

67"In this very hard time that fate has brought, even this little fort, the one fate has shown us, will bring success in the fight.

68"Proud men, fighting with pride in this Shirval, we will give our heads here, but in battle we will not give up our fame.

69–73"For fame the ten-headed one's enemy gave up his beloved wife; for fame Bali, lord of the demons, went to the underworld; for fame the enemy of the demons took on the body of a tortoise; for fame Shibi cut off his own flesh and gave it; for fame the axe-bearing Khanda drank the halahala at once; for fame Dadhichi cut out his own bones and gave them away; for fame Kashyapi gave away the whole earth; for fame Svarnadi's son lay on a bed of arrows. So today, for fame and for gain, we will fight our enemies until they strike us down, picking out the best of us one by one."

74When Ballal had spoken thus, his warriors by the thousand at once took refuge in the fort and roared like elephants.

75Then Kavaji, eager for battle, seeing the enemy warriors take refuge in the rampart, said this to his troops:

Kavaji said:

76"Ah! This strong Ballal, who came to Shiva's country wanting to conquer it, is sheltering behind a rampart.

77"Taking refuge in this Shirval of ours, ah, the dull-witted man shows the measure of his own wit.

78"It has no bastions, no moat as big as its name suggests. So, soldiers, do not think this fort hard to take.

79"Surround it; block every road around it; and in a single moment fill the whole ditch.

80"Make your horses leap like birds over it, or break it open with pickaxes.

81"Dig it up; quickly dig out its foundations. Is this fort Lanka, that we should fear it?

82Told this loudly by Kavaji, the soldiers, roused to battle, at once surrounded that fort on every side and began the assault.

83Seeing these before them, of a valour beyond the gods, the bowmen inside took the start of battle for a festival and began to shoot.

84Whoever raised his head above to look at them had it cut off at once and became like the headless planet Ketu.

85As lines of bees fly from the branches of trees, sharp arrows came out from the bows of the heroes.

86Fierce, very strong men, drawing their bows to the ear, cut off the heads of heroes with sharp arrows.

87Arrows loosed by sure hands fell and went so deep into the ground that the lord of serpents below saw them.

88–89The men on the rampart showered the enemy heroes with wheels, iron bars, pestles, mortars, stones, millstones, burning firebrands, heaps of khadira coals, boiling oil and many kinds of weapons.

90Though struck by the enemy, Shiva's troops, raising their weapons, surrounded the fort and looked all the finer for it.

91Then some soldiers in their fury broke into the fort in many places with long maces and iron bars.

92Some made holes in it with lance-thrusts; some climbed up ladders and attacked the wall.

93Some, mounted on horses swift as Garuda, looking all around, tried hard to leap over it.

94And Kavaji smashed the gateway with heavy blows of maces and other weapons.

95When the hero burst through the gateway and entered the enclosure, Adil's army came at him and fought there.

96The strong Ballal, meeting that onrush like the submarine fire, shone as he took the attack and struck back.

97–98Very handsome, tall, armour fastened on, young, carrying lance and bow, steady, surrounded by his soldiers, riding a tall horse, he shone the more, they say, like a lamp at the moment it goes out.

99–100Then Bhika and Bhima, Tuka, Goda and Sada, Sambha and other fine warriors, with Kavaji at their head, their tall horses leaping quickly and weapons in their hands, struck Ballal and his men with fierce anger.

101Then, in that blinding darkness of hatred, the weapons of the fighters on both sides clashed against one another.

102–103As the riders of horses swifter than Garuda rushed at one another in the fury of battle, weapons struck weapons by the hundred, like flashes of lightning striking flashes of lightning in the sky.

104Lance-bearer struck lance-bearer, bowman bowman, mace-man mace-man, and was then himself cut to pieces.

105Shield-bearer fought only shield-bearer, armoured man only armoured man, bowman only bowman in that battle.

106Arrows that could not pierce the armour and flew quickly upward shone for a moment in the sky like the rays of the sun.

107When arrows pierced the armour of warriors and entered the earth, streams of blood began to flow one after another.

108There the headless trunks of men whose heads had been cut off by arrows from angry hands spurted blood and bellowed.

109Then the trunks of elephants and the necks of horses, cut through by arrows, fell to the ground.

110Hosts of heroes bearing bows scattered many enemy heads, cut off by arrows, all over the field of battle.

111One man rushed at another who came at him mace in hand and seized that crocodile of the ocean of battle.

112One who had lost his own hand there, cut off, attacked enemies like an elephant with his one remaining hand.

113–114In a moment the Ingales killed twenty-five warriors there, the Poles five and seven, the Chors fourteen, the Ghatges nine and eight, the Waghs sixteen, and Kavaji killed nineteen fine warriors.

115Then a river of blood flowed swiftly there, rising from the bodies of many foot soldiers, horses and elephants killed in the fight.

116When the enemy heroes had beaten it and surrounded it by force, Ballal's army fled the battle in fear.

117Haibat's son could not steady his army there, turned back by the enemy and wavering.

118Then, they say, full of rivalry, raising his weapon, he charged the enemy swiftly in anger, as Vritra charged the immortals.

119As many arrows as were in his quiver, so many foremost men of Kavaji's did he bring down.

120Just as he raised his lance to frighten the enemies around him, Kavaji struck him down with a blow of his lance.

121–122When Haibat's son, fighting with pride, had been felled by the commander of Shiva's army as a lion fells a maddened elephant, and the ground was smeared with blood, fat, marrow and flesh, no one in his army could keep steady.

123Then the proud Kavaji let go free the hundreds of people who sought refuge, holding grass in their teeth.

124Some, fighting in the pride of battle, rushed forward, were cut to pieces by arrows and went to Shatakratu's realm.

125–126Some with feet cut off, some with hands cut off, some with armour cut through, some with chests cut open, some with hips broken, some with elbows broken, rolled on the ground crying piteously and fell senseless.

127–129Then, while the enemy lay on the field of battle, the fine warriors Kavaji and the others gladly took his elephants, tall horses, ornaments of many kinds, bright clothes, armour, weapons, litters, treasure and other equipment, and went to the hill of Purandar to see their lord, who was like Purandara.

130Having killed the enemy heroes and swiftly finished their task, Kavaji and the other soldiers bowed with bent heads before the son of Shah, presenting the many fine horses taken from the enemy heroes, a wealth of gold, strings of pearls and jewels.

Thus ends the thirteenth canto in the Samhita, the Anupurana brought to light by Kavindra Paramananda.

Musekhan killed; Fatte Khan flees

The prince of poets said:

1Hearing that Haibat's strong son had been struck down in battle, the proud Fateh Khan was furious with King Shiva.

2–3Then, surrounded by strong Muslims led by Muse Khan, guarded by kings led by Mattaji, and with vassals like rut-maddened elephants all around him, he quickly set out, wanting to conquer King Shiva.

4–8Then that guardian of Adil's earth, eager for battle, quickly gathered and saw before him, at Purandar, an ocean of an army: crowded with the crocodiles of moving, rut-maddened elephants; its waves the lines of tall, restless, leaping horses; glittering with the billows of many banners shaken by the wind; full of clouds of dust stirred from the face of the earth; its shores echoing the deep boom of rolling kettledrums; pale with a foam of white parasols; its tortoises hard shields; its serpents fierce bows; bringing terror like the submarine fire with its flashing weapons.

9The proud Fateh Khan, moving forward, camped his mighty army not far from that hill.

10Seeing the enemy army come near, the son of Shah had the war-drum beaten on the summit of the hill.

11With the sound of that drum he at once shook the minds of the enemy soldiers, as the wind shakes the Manasa lake.

12–14Then the proud Fateh Khan, surrounded by Muse Khan and other heroes of matching quality and great arrogance, bearing fine swords like the fire of the world's end, with great zeal for war, armoured, mighty, steady, roaring their war cries, at once began to climb the hill of Purandar.

15–16As that great army climbed, splitting the clouds with its roars, Fateh Khan was at the rear, Muse Khan at the front, the lord of Phaltan on one flank and the Ghatge on the other.

17Those who had never set foot on the ground, and always went in vehicles, climbed that high hill then, alas, on their own feet.

18Seeing the enemy climbing the hill on every side, the commanders of Shiva's army roared like angry lions.

19–20Shiva's brave men by the thousand showered the enemy heavily with masses of burning iron that shot out with a roar, with musket-balls, with many great boulders, with hundreds of fire-arrows and with many sling-stones.

21–24Great boulders broken from the cliffs, which Shiva's troops, roaring, hurled down — falling from far above, smashing as they struck, raising dust like rain clouds, shooting out showers of sparks as they broke apart — burst into pieces, flew up all around and fell hard on the enemy army.

25–26Then, by many iron balls that shot from the cannon, flaming, unbearable and terrifying from afar like lightning, Adil's soldiers were cut to pieces, and like birds they flew up into the sky and fed the lines of falcons there.

27–28At that moment, fire-arrows with their marks, roaring terribly as they fell from the sky, like serpents with mouths blazing with poison-flame, whirled and whirled and burned the circling army of the Muslims.

29There a single ball shot from a cannon pierced many Muslims, and they fell to the ground.

30Some, their chests crushed by the stones thrown by the men on the hill, fainted and stayed where they were, halfway up the path.

31–32Some, stuck to the hillside and broken by huge stones, vomited a great deal of blood, red as cochineal. Carried off by their friends who followed them, they turned back to their own camp, wanting to save their lives.

33–34Some, their bodies torn by the balls shot from the guns, covered in spurts of blood like the jets of a fountain, burning with fever and confused with loss of breath, as if thirsty, went to be guests of Death.

35Seeing that army beaten by the enemy, Muse Khan spoke to the warriors of his own race:

Muse Khan said:

36"These falls of huge rocks — are they not portents at every step? Falling from every side, they are striking down the captains of our army.

37"Ah! How great is the rise of this lord of Purandar, that he himself overpowers even valiant men like us.

38"For us, whose fame in the world was born of the sword, it would be the greatest shame to run away from here.

39"Do not set your foot backward. Look ahead at the mountain. Victory hardly ever abandons the man who stands firm in battle.

40–42Saying this, the great-minded man, with men who never turn back, climbed the hill of Purandar. Then Sharfa Shah with many soldiers of his own, the two Shaikhs, King Mattaji, the strong lord of Phaltan, Fateh Khan's troops and many vassals came on swiftly.

43–44Seeing that great wave climbing the slope of Purandar like masses of thundering clouds, at once, at their master's command, Goda and the other very swift warriors raised their weapons and met them in every direction.

45Muse Khan and the others too fearlessly attacked those enemy fighters, as planets attack great planets.

46–47Then Sadoji, maddened by the strength of his arm, fought Sharfa Shah; Jagtap, born of a family that sets the world right, fought the angry Muse Khan; Bhairav fought Mina and Ratan; and Wagh, who wielded swift weapons, fought the heroic Ghatge.

48The very proud Kavaji, mace in hand, with a strong force, fought many other soldiers.

49–51Then, filled with the pride of battle, with arms long as yokes, their bodies covered with fine helmets and armour, warriors in both armies, hostile to each other, brandishing their bows and setting sharp arrows to them, crying again and again "Wait, wait!", fought a wonderful fight on the slope of Purandar.

52As they struck one another there for Purandar, alas, many could not get down from it, and many could not get up.

53Watching one another and taking aim, some covered the earth with arrows and others the sky.

54The arrow that pierced one man's chest and cut off another's arm took off a third man's head, helmet and all.

55There Goda, wild with rage, rushed in and drove his sharp lance into Muse Khan's chest.

56–57But the Muslim, with both hands, pulled out from his own chest that sharp, terrible lance which the enemy had driven in, piercing his armour, and in rage, biting his lips with his teeth, at once broke it in two.

58The chest, wet with blood, adorned him there as a slab of rock reddened with thick red ochre adorns a mountain.

59Meanwhile the steady fighter Sadoji, carrying shield and sword, came at Sharfa Shah to fight him.

60–62Both mad for battle, both red-eyed, both armed, both full of zeal, both whirling their swords and lighting up the sky like lightning, both watching for an opening in the other, roaring like lions, showing the moves called bhranta, udbhranta and the rest, the two of them shone.

63Then, their bodies wounded by each other's sword strokes, in a single moment both were drenched with blood.

64Then Wagh, a tiger among men, struck the Ghatge, who carried a terrible spear, on his pillar-like arm with a very sharp spear.

65Then that Ghatge, skilled in war, his body smeared with flowing blood, struck the tiger hard on the arm with a spear.

66Struck by him, Wagh, like a second tiger, showed his own valour and delighted his own men.

67Mina and Ratan, drawing their bows to the ear, came at the fearsome Bhairav Chor and covered him with arrows.

68His body, riddled by the arrows those two shot, looked like a sieve.

69Though he was one and they were two, the battle there went on for a long time; yet Bhairav Chor won victory at every step.

70Kavaji, enraged and blazing like fire, laid low hundreds of enemies with great blows of his mace.

71–72Their fine armour shattered, their chests torn open, suddenly fainting, their weapons slipping from their hands, reddening the ground with blood flowing from every limb, the men who fell on that battlefield looked splendid.

73Then those fine warriors, their limbs red with flowing blood, shone like mountains wet with red ochre carried by their streams.

74Shiva too, the hero radiant with heroic splendour, watched that marvel of a battle between his own men and the enemy.

75Then from the slope of Purandar a great river of red water began to flow, roaring, wild with pride.

76Mounted in the sky in their aerial cars and lighting up the heavens, all the gods watched that great battle again and again and praised it.

77Hara came there gladly with his goblins, lovely with a garland of heads that he had made at that moment.

78The flesh-eating rakshasas, greatly hungry at that moment, found plenty of flesh and were satisfied.

79Then a ghost, setting a skull fearlessly on its lap, quickly drank its sweet drink mixed with blood.

80Then the shakinis, together with the dakinis skilled in leaping and dancing about, fed their own bodies on the flesh of the soldiers.

The prince of poets said:

81Now, Brahmins, I shall tell you everything — how Goda killed Muse Khan there. Listen.

82When the strong Muse Khan had broken his lance, Goda, enraged, took up a sword shaped like the belly of a black crow.

83Wielding that lightning of a sword, he cut down Pathans there by the hundred, and they fell to the ground in pieces.

84But Muse Khan, seeing that superhuman deed of his, struck him on the left arm with his own sword.

85Though struck on the arm by the swift Muse Khan, the great hero did not tremble, as a forest tree does not tremble in the wind.

86–87Their brows knitted, their eyes very angry, their hands with their terrible creeper-like swords going and coming, roaring like elephants and striking each other, their helmets and armour shattered, the two of them shone.

88Then, cut by each other's weapons, both together sprinkled the earth with streams of blood.

89Ah! For a moment the battle between those two was even; then, through the wound he had been dealt, Goda gained the upper hand.

90Then, though shaken by many blows, the strong Muse Khan brought his sword down on Goda's head.

91Before his sword could fall on Goda's head, Goda struck that enemy with his own sword.

92Struck then by Goda, wrath in bodily form, he was split from the shoulder to the middle into two halves.

93His body, cut in two, fell, and the ground turned red with the gushing of his blood.

94When Muse Khan had been struck down by the scion of the Jagtap family, who set the world right, Muslims by the hundred became guests of Yama there.

95Unable to fight Sado sword against sword, the enemy drew back and, out of pride, took up a fine bow.

96–97Then Sharfa Shah struck him with sharp arrows, drawing a bow that bent into a ring to the edge of the horizon, many-coloured like the bow of Indra. Sado, sword in hand, came at him and cut the bow.

98–99When his quick-handed foe had cut in two that bow, bright with many colours and set with silver points, he threw away the useless bow and at once took up a terrible iron club to kill him.

100Sado, roaring like a lion, cut that weapon of his the moment he had taken hold of it.

101Whatever weapon the Muslim took in his hand, Sado, in the heat of heroism, cut it at once.

102Then, weaponless, turning his face away, the Muslim took the road of flight as best he could.

103Then the Ghatge too, after fighting that enemy warrior for a long time, quickly fled from him, as one elephant flees another.

104Mina and Ratan too, taking Bhairav for Bhairava himself, slipped away from that battle, which was like the end of the world.

105Fateh Khan did not look at his army as it turned back from Purandar seeking safety; he lost heart and turned his face away.

106When the son of King Shah had broken by force in battle Fateh Khan, who had come against him with a great army, the hero, his course unchecked through the favour of fate, his valour ever rising, alas, turned to conquer the lord of the earth at Vijayapura.

107Badly beaten in battle by King Shiva, Fateh Khan in dejection went back to Vijayapura. Hearing that news in the assembly, Mahmud at once sank, his mind long tormented, in an ocean of anxiety.

Thus ends the fourteenth canto, called "The Killing of Muse Khan", in the Samhita, the Anupurana brought to light by Kavindra Paramananda.

Shahaji released

The prince of poets said:

1–2Hearing that Muse Khan had been struck down by the enemy on the slope of Purandar, that the large army had been driven back by superior valour, and that Fateh Khan had turned tail and come back, Mahmud found no joy by day or night.

3Then Mahmud, grieving inwardly at Fateh Khan's defeat, thought long in his own mind:

4"Ah! These kshatriyas, whom we have protected day and night, now that their time has risen, are throwing us Muslims down.

5"Having gained greater wealth by sheltering with me, this bold King Shah, mad with pride, does not respect my command.

6"This angry man, waging a fierce war, defeated Randulla near the town of Pundarika.

7"By the art of his counsel he brought Malik Ambar under his control, and he humbled the unruly lord of Anegal.

8"Even the Nizam, who had long protected him as a father would, he thoroughly deceived with his clever tricks.

9"Alas, through this man's counsel the kings of Karnataka have given up obeying me and proclaim that they belong to him.

10"Though called again and again, he never came near me. This man has thrown our victory into a pit of doubt.

11"He helped Ibrahim before at every step, and Ibrahim, pleased, raised him to the highest rank.

12"For that reason I put up, day after day, with hundreds of his offences as he went very far astray.

13"But when he committed great offences I could not forgive, I ordered Mustafa Khan to seize him.

14"Now, with him in custody, his sons Shambhu and Shiva, unruly and eager to sink me, are making war.

15"For their father's sake, Shambhu has beaten Farad there, and here Shiva has put Fateh Khan to flight in battle.

16"Enough! How great is the strength of famous Shambhu! It is not Farad who was broken there; it is my mind that has been broken today.

17"Shiva, in whose hands are the hills of Simhagad and Purandar today — how will he not make me pay?

18"The hill of Purandar, where the fearsome battle took place, terrible as Bhishma, is very hard for us to take.

19"This Shah has placed Shambhu in the city of Bengrul and Shiva on the hill of Purandar. How can he be beaten by us?

20"If I do not release the father of Shambhu and Shiva, alas, I must give up this rich fortune of mine.

21"If I release him like a serpent set free from its jar, then, harmful as he is, will he not harm me?

22"'He must be released' is one counsel; 'he must not be released' the other. Of the two, I think the first is for my good.

23"In any case, by the power of my counsel, the anger of this crooked man, harmful though he is, will be completely calmed.

24"I shall do him this favour and lay it on his head. Being of good family and foremost in virtue, he will not forget it."

25After thinking long in his mind, the wise Adil told this to his ministers, and they too thought it right.

26–27Then Mahmud had Shah brought before him — Shah who, having taken the auspicious bath and wearing spotless clothes and ornaments, shone like a new sun freed from its halo — seated him in a seat befitting his honour, comforted him, and spoke to him with joy:

Mahmud said:

28"Know that what happened was done without my knowing. Nothing in the world is hard for a wise king like you to understand.

29"Whatever offence, great or small, Mustafa or Afzal committed out of hatred, count it as mine, lord of kings.

30"I wanted to see you, and for that I made many efforts. Alas, I am delighted. Whether you are pleased or not is up to you.

31"You have been spoken to with gentle words and released from custody. You are well born, O king: be eager to do what I wish.

32"The great-souled are satisfied with even a particle of help and do not remember even a hundred wrongs.

33"Those of small mind, upset by even a tiny wrong, do not remember thousands of kindnesses.

34"The wicked man does a thousand wrongs; the good man only does a thousand kindnesses.

35"Those who remember the kindness done by others and not the kindness they have done are the ones who keep faith and are grateful, in the judgement of the good.

36"The king who does not help people intent on helping him finds even great wealth as illusory as nectar found in a dream.

37"Instruct your younger son, proud as a mountain, and give me my Simha hill, great king.

38"My resolve to take the Simha hill will never leave me. By my leave, let Purandar remain Shiva's.

39"And let Shambhu too, who routed Farad and made him flee, give me the city of Bengrul as a present.

40"Masters engage servants fit for the work, moment by moment, for tasks of every kind. If the servants do not do it, what is the point of their keeping their good name?

41"The master appoints for his own ends; the servant serves for his own ends. If either fails in his part, neither's work is done.

42"As there is no pleasure without a meal and no body without life, so, great king, a servant does not shine without following his master.

43"What use is saying much? Now I am yours and you are mine. Our mutual reliance is the support of the people."

44In this way Adil made King Shah hear a speech of few syllables but great meaning.

45Whenever Mahmud, the self-contradicting talker, made some point, Shah gave his answer with a half-smile of his own.

46The great-minded man then thought that Mahmud's demand, born of pride, for the giving of those forts, was a fetter on his own feet.

47Then Adil had elephants and horses tied at the door of the house of the released and duly honoured Shah.

48Then he, intent on the joy of the world, was freed from that great danger, like the moon from a covering of massed clouds.

49Freed from that captivity, the friend of all the world shone the brighter, like the sun freed from an eclipse.

50As soon as he was freed, he gathered a great army, and all the earth shone with the splendour of his sons.

51Then the strong-armed Shambhu, at his father's command, which could not be disobeyed, at once gave up the city of Bengrul.

52And Shiva, though able to fight and though it was in no way to be given up, gave up the Simha hill for the sake of his revered father.

53Then, honoured by Adil, who called him his own with words of respect and gratified him with presents at every step, King Shah, sent by Adil with a great army gathered, set out to conquer his enemies.

Thus ends the fifteenth canto in the Samhita, the Anupurana brought to light by Kavindra Paramananda.

Sonopant's statecraft

The prince of poets said:

1–2Then, when Mahmud was greatly content at getting what he wanted, but Shiva had himself given up the Simha hill for his father's sake, Shiva, skilled in counsel, called near him the Brahmin Sonaji, his elder in birth and a knower of counsel, and said:

Shiva said:

3"Surely that great-minded great king, whom people know to be blessed and the foremost of men who know, does not really know me.

4"If an ignorant man does not know a good man's virtues, he is merely unfortunate. But if a man who knows does not know them, that pains the good man's heart.

5"If he knew me, he would not have given away the Simha hill. Otherwise, who could have taken by force this fort that was in my hand?

6"What shall I say of that great-souled man, who, though no fool, gave the Simha hill to Adil, his enemy?

7"Though he himself governs everyone, alas, trusting his enemies he slept at ease in his own house, and then was seized in his own house by his enemies.

8"An enemy should never be despised even if he is weak. A burning lamp can be put out even by a moth.

9"A moth dies even as it destroys the lamp; who but a man born blind would see the two as equal?

10"Surely even an ant can enter an elephant's trunk and be the death of it. So an enemy should never be thought small.

11"Ah! This strong-armed man, though he knows great policy, simply ignores his enemies. That is his great fault.

12–13"I am fully ready to take vengeance for the long suffering in prison of my revered father Shah, who was captured by the enemy. From today my fame will spread through the destruction of the Muslims.

14"The enemy is afraid even of the man who is greatly afraid of him; it goes without saying that the enemy fears the man who does not fear him at all.

15"Surely I must kill those Muslim captains. These arrows of mine, sharpened on the whetstone, are eager for it.

16"My mind is now eager to take the city called Vijaya. So I tell you today: have the horsemen made ready at once."

Sonaji said:

17"Enmity with the strong ends badly; a store of sin ends badly; reproach of the revered and of elders ends badly.

18"Your father made an enemy of the strong and acted stubbornly. Since he was not careful, his capture was what he deserved.

19"That great king is mighty, and his ministers are men of good conduct; yet the enmity he made with the strong was against policy.

20"When a weak man makes enmity with a strong one, the strong man is fully able to defeat him.

21"Gifts are prescribed for an enemy who is stronger, conciliation for an equal, punishment for one who is weaker; division is held to apply to all.

22"Through division an equal enemy, growing weaker day by day, gives up his equality, and a stronger enemy his superiority. Who would not praise division?

23"O king, division is right even against a weaker enemy, for it makes the weak weaker still, which is all to the good in war.

24"If a weak enemy is conciliated in the thought that he is suffering, he swells up and thinks himself great.

25"O king, the strength of the weak and the weakness of the strong are seen to come about through the power of changing times.

26"So a king who keeps in mind the view of Varuna, lord of the waters, punishes a weak enemy.

27"Means are held to be of two kinds, effective and ineffective. The second is barren; the first bears fruit.

28"Women take away a man's strength; wine brings delusion; gambling takes away wealth; avarice ruins one's work.

29"Harsh speech brings ill fortune; hunting is sinful; and harsh punishment, lord of men, brings very great disgrace.

30"So one should give up these seven vices, for even one of them, alas, leads to ruin of judgement.

31"If even one of them ruins judgement, what man who indulges in many of them will not go to the depths?

32"Fortune abandons the man who is averse to the shastras of policy, lazy in the weight of his own affairs, and whose mind is sunk in vice.

33"When he grows careless, the treasurers loudly rob his treasury, and evil-minded officials oppress his realm.

34"Delusion grows in the king addicted to vices, and as delusion grows, his sense of what is fitting greatly declines.

35"As his sense of the fitting declines, all people despise him, and those appointed to tasks do not quickly carry them out.

36"Then, angry, he utters harsh words loudly and wounds even those gathered in his assembly day after day.

37"Then, their hearts wounded by insult moment after moment, they speak ill of him behind his back and mock him.

38–39"Then some leave him, some grow indifferent to him, some go over to the enemy's side and live there at ease, some, thinking him short-tempered, curse him, and some who do not leave him no longer serve him as before.

40"Through attachment to the seven vices, not knowing his enemy's deceit or strength, he thinks himself mighty out of mere conceit.

41"Alas! He whose mind is attached to vices becomes like this, and when he marches out for victory he is at once overcome by enemies.

42"Hills are not heavy; a father alone is held heavy. That is why, mighty as you are, you gave up the Simha hill.

43"If that lord of the earth is released once it has been given to this enemy, then, though you gave the Simha hill, it is not truly given.

44"What has that enemy, who thinks the Simha hill and the city of Bengrul equal to the Meru that is Shah, really gained?

45"You have now begun on the circle of conquest, and to a king who strides out boldly the whole earth belongs.

46"The king, then the minister, friends, ample wealth, the realm, forts and armies: these are the seven limbs of a kingdom.

47"A kingdom with all these limbs complete is praised by the good; if it lacks even one, it is mocked by others.

48–49"The king is the head; the minister the mouth; wealth and army the two arms; the realm the body; the friends the strong joints; the forts the strong bones. Thus the wise have named the seven limbs of the kingdom.

50–53"Of this seven-limbed kingdom, dharma is called the soul; counsel the breath; policy the strength and impolicy the weakness; proper punishment the courage; adharma the disease; the enemy's insolence the intoxication; mutual division the long life; unity the beloved company of the people; foresight the eye; valour the bright form; a clear mind the life; enmity with the strong the obstacle; heedlessness the sleep; and vigilance the waking.

54"Enmity is said to be of two kinds, equal and unequal: the first with an equal, the second with a stronger.

55"In equal enmity neither side can truly win, being equal. In unequal enmity victory surely goes to the stronger, because he is stronger.

56"Because of Fateh Khan's rout and Muse Khan's killing, Mahmud, lord, envies you day and night.

57"See, O king: Adil and the lord of Delhi, Muslims next to your own country and strong, plot against you day and night.

58"Surely for the man who has these two hostile neighbours on both sides, sitting still in this place is not fitting now.

59"So, lord of the world, take your stand in a very inaccessible place and strive to conquer the world. What is unconquerable for you, Shiva?

60"Kailasa was taken by the destroyer of Kandarpa, Meru by the bearer of the thunderbolt, and the waters of the ocean by the conqueror of the demons, for shelter.

61"People do not count a fort impregnable merely because it is a fort. Its impregnability lies in its master being impregnable.

62"A fort is made impregnable by its master, and a master by his fort. Without both, an enemy becomes impregnable.

63"Have all the forts you possess at once made as impregnable as possible in every way."

64Hearing these words of Sonaji, that best of princes, worthy of honour even from the very great, held them worthy of honour.

65Then, holding sovereignty for the protection of the three worlds, hater of his father's enemies, the younger son of Shah, that jewel, fearless, intent on great slaughter of the Muslims, thought of the whole earth as resting in the palm of his hand.

Thus ends the sixteenth canto in the Anupurana brought to light by Kavindra Paramananda of Nivasa.

The prince of poets said:

1Then Ali Shah called Afzal, the mainstay of his army, and himself spoke words suited to the occasion.

Ali Shah said:

2"In this host of soldiers you are the one who does us good: a hater of Brahmins and gods, like a second Kali, the age of Time.

3"Setting out before at the head of an army, you defeated in battle the kings born of the line of Ramaraja.

4"While your valour, which scorches enemies, is awake, alas, even Shriranga Raja has lost his taste for the stage of battle.

5"You, a hero, by your valour brought the lord of Karnapura under control in an instant, as a snake-charmer subdues a hissing serpent.

6"Strong as Mount Mandara, you churned the city of Madhura, took the city of Kanchi, and carried off its gold.

7"Even Virabhadra, made your servant at every step by your splendour, has forgotten the riches of parasol and chowrie.

12"Alas, through that zealous, proud hero, who is devoted to his own dharma, the dharma of the Muslims is being destroyed.

13"Overrunning the land step by step like a lion overrunning a terrible forest, this man, who is his own master, takes no account of my command.

20"A march that would take a day and night, a fortnight or a month, this fearless man covers in a moment.

21"Deceitful by nature from his very boyhood, wakeful and of fierce valour, he scorns the Muslims.

22"This man of great valour attacked the cities of the copper-faced men and punished Ahmadnagar and the other cities fiercely.

23"Even the lord of Delhi, though he has with great effort secured the lasting possession of the Nizam's kingdom, does not think it safe for fear of him.

26"Though young, he does not shrink from fear of enemies, and he outdoes us with astonishing valour.

27"Growing day by day in valour and fortune, he is sought out by kings who desire riches.

29"The heroes I sent again and again to conquer him did not come back once they met that valiant man.

30"He who makes an end of enemy heroes and fears nothing — I see no one but you who can conquer him.

34"You have today roused, by telling me the task, the power that lies awake in me to destroy the enemy's ranks.

35"In sending this servant out on a fierce war, I think you have yourself shown him favour and made him your own.

36"If a master does not tell his servants what to do, who will ever know whether they have valour or not?

41–45fine horses with jewelled saddles and leaping gait, elephants of the noble breed with their ornaments, armour, helmets, weapons of many kinds, bright clothes, his own titles, many litters that put the gods' cars to shame, couches of silver and of gold, ewers and spittoons, jewelled turban ornaments, pearl necklaces, armlets and the like, bracelets and girdles set with many gems, many fine elephants of noble breed from beyond the seas, and many treasure-chests: these Afzal received from Ali Shah.

51–54Ambar, equal to Shambara; the valiant Yakut; the very proud Muse Khan; Hasan the Pathan; Randulla's son, also called Randulla; Ankush Khan, whose gait was like an unchecked elephant's; Khelkarna's Abyssinian, who had been bought as a son; the strong-armed Hilal, an elephant against enemy trees — these and other Muslims, with their armies and kinsmen, at their master's command quickly followed that general.

60Birds that went far on the left, crying loudly, seemed to tell him that his daring was in vain.

61–64Alas, though it was midday, the sun shone dimly; the sky seemed ablaze and the quarters grew grey with dust; a great meteor suddenly fell from the sky; there was a crash of thunder from a sky without clouds; terrifying jackals howled from the eastern quarter; his banner broke; the vehicles were distressed; a wind blew contrary, raining stones and dust; and the elephant at the head of the army, though checked by the goad, ran wild.

65Though these and other portents warned him, he, like Namuchi, did not give up his eagerness for battle.

66Then, having set out from the city called Vijaya, he made his camp at a place half a yojana away.

67Having come there with armies gathered on every side, he shone in his camp like an ocean of an army.

5That mother of King Shiva, living on Rajgiri, was devoted to guarding her own mountain forts.

6–7Then the general Netaji, attended by many armies, having burned the whole of Shahpur and turned his back on the enemy, received Shiva's message as if it were a favour, and came with Hilal to Shivapattana.

8–9Then he saw on Rajgiri the queen of King Shah, loving, long eager to see her child, ready to go herself to the hill of Pranala, given over to anger, and surrounded by her own great army.

10As if he had done some wrong, frightened at every step, he bowed with Hilal to that lady of great vows.

11Seeing those two bowing with folded hands, the fortunate lady said in a soft and deep voice:

The queen mother said:

13"Ah! There you two have left your master, abandoned all shame, and turned back out of fear of the enemy!

14"To free that child of mine, I myself will strive, and today I will take Johar's head in battle."

15"Without him this quarter seems empty to me, like a forest. I shall bring back at once that child of mine, risen up like a lion.

16"If for a moment I do not see that only son of mine, a portion of the one marked with the Shrivatsa, I shall give up my life.

17"All of you, together, fight the enemy here. I myself will fight that enemy Johar."

The general said:

20"So, at his command, I went and conquered Vijayapattana, and I have come back — all good to you — to fight the copper-faced men.

22"When shall I see that lord of mine, the leader of the invincible? And when shall I join battle with the enemy?

26As he quickly mounted his horse, sword in hand and firm, armies of six kinds followed him, the jewel of a great line.

27That strong-armed man led a great army, its banners grey with the dust shaken up by the trunks of the elephants.

28–29Johar, hearing that an enemy army had come, guarded by the general and by Hilal, sent there many warriors of his own, bearing spears and broadswords, to oppose it.

30–31Then all those Abyssinians, full of great pride, bearing spears, nooses, bows and arrows, bold in action, blocked in the middle of the road that very great and rich army of the lord of the mountain, raised their weapons, and fought it.

37Not even Hilal could free that son of his, his very self, as it were, as the enemies led him away.

The king said:

57"This is the fifth month that has come, the auspicious Shravana, dark with clouds, spreading great beauty here over the forest-filled region.

58"Ah! Johar, standing at the foot of this mountain, whom we of irresistible valour should have killed — he has not been killed by us. That is a wonder.

78Then, having crossed the road, he climbed his own mountain, Vishalgiri, broad as a pillar of the earth and fine to see, and made the desired stay there, a cause of rest for his army.

From Vishalgad to Rajgad

The learned men said:

1–3When he had come out of the hill of Pranala, a fort made impregnable by the army he left there, with six hundred foot soldiers and unseen, how did Johar, bewildered by the goddess's yogic maya, come to know of it? 3To him who had angrily taken away the wealth of Rajapur and conquered Shringarpur by his might, hundreds upon hundreds of the best of the enemy's soldiers, seeking refuge, bowed.

4Then, seeing for a moment a famous hill lying in his own country, fit for the protection of that town, he gave it the name Pratit (Pratitgad).

5Then the summer season, bringing joy with camphor, lotus stalks, moonlight, jasmine, sandalwood, champak and the rest, served that man of great joy.

6Wearing the bow of the kesara flower, with a quiver of patali blossoms and arrows of flowers, the god of love, though he conquers the three worlds, did not steal Shiva's heart at all.

7In the hot season, when the sun burned fiercely, the people, in pain, took shelter at the rivers, whose water, though quickly made lukewarm by the sun, kept cool beneath.

8The fierce summer, burning hotter and hotter, drying up day by day the water of the ponds, left the cranes sitting in the mud and the rows of fish and tortoises dying.

9With the breeze filled with the fragrance of the blossoming shirisha and patali, even the minds of the wise, though free of the objects of sense, were troubled by the fever of love.

Here the text breaks off. The manuscript ends at this point, and nothing more of the poem is known.
