Perversion of India's Political Parlance

by Sita Ram Goel · Voice of India

A short polemic about political vocabulary. Goel argues that India's everyday political labels, such as Left and Right, secular and communal, progressive and reactionary, were not born in the freedom struggle. In his account they were imported from Soviet Communism in the 1920s and 30s, spread by Nehru and the Congress Socialists, and have since been used to cast Hindu nationalism as the villain. He ends by calling for a new political language rooted in Sanatana Dharma.

This page sets out what the book claims, in the author's terms and attributed to him, so you can follow the argument. It does not endorse or fact-check those claims, and it leaves out the book's harsher language about Muslims and Christians.

Chapter 1 · the opening story

J.P. visits an RSS camp, 1959

Goel was then secretary of an organisation led by Jayaprakash Narayan. An RSS leader asked him to invite J.P. to a camp in New Delhi. J.P. was offended at first. Goel told him that refusing to meet one group while meeting everyone else amounted to “practising untouchability.” J.P. went, spent two hours there and was impressed by the young, educated volunteers. On the drive back he still said he suspected “an attempt to revive the Maratha empire.”

“I felt very strongly that there was something seriously wrong somewhere.”Goel on what the visit showed him. The rest of the book is his answer: the problem lies in the political vocabulary.
Chapter 2 · words which defy dictionaries

Six word pairs, two meanings each

Goel looks up each label in the dictionary and compares that meaning with how it is used in Indian politics. His claim is that one side gave itself the flattering word (blue, left) and gave its opponents the damning one (orange, right), with no neutral referee. Open a pair to see the comparison.

Chapter 4 · the character of Leftist language

Four “languages of imperialism” that reached India

Goel's central idea is that every imperial ideology speaks the same language with different words. He lists four that reached India and dates each arrival. In his view the Communist one is the latest and is now spoken by the Left.

Spans follow the book: Arab and Turkish armies “from the middle of the 7th to the middle of the 18th century”, the Portuguese from the early 16th century, British rule for about 150 years, Communism from November 1917.

The same eight moves

One pattern, four vocabularies

Goel says each imperial language makes the same eight moves. Read across a row to compare the four versions he gives.

Chapters 3, 5 and 6 · the history

How the vocabulary changed, 1857–1959

Goel divides the freedom struggle into stages, each with its own vocabulary. In his account the Left–Right vocabulary arrived only after 1917, stayed within a small circle until the late 1930s, and became dominant after 1947.

“Conveyor belts”

How the language spread, in Goel's telling

Goel argues the Communist Party alone could not have spread this vocabulary. It took carriers inside the national movement and in the universities, and above all Nehru.

Chapter 7 · the place of Mahatma Gandhi

Gandhi, criticised and praised

Goel treats Gandhi as the last great speaker of the nationalist language. He also blames him for his approach to the Muslim question. He then argues that the Hindu leaders who came before Gandhi made the same mistakes.

Where Goel faults him
  • Treated Islam as a religion equal to Hinduism
  • Backed the Khilafat agitation to win Muslim support
  • Courted Mohammad Ali and Jinnah despite their hostility
  • Discouraged efforts to organise Hindus
  • Could not prevent Partition
Where Goel praises him
  • Stirred Hindu masses in a way the purely political leaders did not
  • Spoke the language of Sanatana Dharma (Hind Swaraj, Young India)
  • Defended temples, cow protection and varnashrama
  • Staked his life on ending untouchability
  • Placed beside Dayananda, Vivekananda, Tilak and Aurobindo

Goel lists earlier concessions to show the failure came before Gandhi: Hindu money for Sir Syed's Aligarh college, the 1916 Lucknow Pact under Tilak, and the June 1920 Khilafat meeting attended by Lajpat Rai, Pal, Malaviya and Rajagopalachari.

“We were one nation before they came to India.”Gandhi, Hind Swaraj, quoted by Goel as the nationalist language he wants revived
Chapter 8 · towards a language of Indian nationalism

What Goel proposes instead

Goel wants a vocabulary drawn from the Mahabharata, the Puranas and the Dharmashastras, and from the Swadeshi-era leaders. It would frame politics as a deva-asura-sangrama, a struggle of light against darkness, and draw these five conclusions.

1 · Territory

Bharatavarsha is one indivisible whole, and its partition into Afghanistan, Pakistan, India and Bangladesh should be undone.

2 · Creeds

Islam, Christianity and Communism should be treated as political ideologies of empire rather than as religions, and denied protection under the name of secularism.

3 · Economy

Reject both capitalism and socialism as forms of centralisation. Revive swadeshi, meaning local control of local resources.

4 · Society

Resist the totalitarian sameness of Communism and consumerism. Keep the variety of India's institutions.

5 · State

Build a strong central state with room for regional autonomy. Goel calls the lack of one “the bane of India's national life.”
