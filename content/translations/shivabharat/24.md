# Canto 24

*The learned men said:*

1–2. When Muse Khan and the others, wanting to escape death, had suddenly turned their backs on that battle, and, stripped of their army and ashamed, had gone to Vijayapattana without Afzal, what did they do, and what did they think?

3. Hearing of Afzal's end and of the taking of the hill of Pranala, what did the utterly foolish Adil himself do next?

4. And what did that hero, conqueror of the world, slayer of Afzal by force, do after he had taken that hill?

*The prince of poets said:*

5–6. Muse Khan, together with Fazil, Yakut, Ankush, Hasan and his own people, went to the city called Vijaya, bowed to his master, and stood before him with bowed neck and folded hands.

7. Seeing him so dejected, his face cast down, Adil roused him greatly with speeches swollen with pride:

*Adil said:*

8. "If Afzal, who gave up his life for his master's business through a sudden act of daring, came to that end, then he did not come to anything to be mourned.

9. "The enemy, himself skilled with weapons and of a crooked nature, called him alone into a lonely forest and killed him.

10. "If he had gone there with a large army, he would not have come to such an end.

11. "Undertakings do not succeed through daring alone; valour joined with policy surely bears great fruit.

12. "He who in anger killed Afzal in that forest is like a serpent lying in one's lap; he must not be ignored.

13. "So I shall root out from the Sahya hills by every means that wretch who has done me this unbearable wrong, like a thorn.

14. "Alas, he has now come from Vairata, and is quickly gathering his armies to come to the hill of Pranala.

15. "The spies here report this rumour. So all of you, marching out, show great valour.

16–17. "Calling those very wakeful leaders of the armies in each region to destroy the enemy, like planets, and the proud soldiers too, for help, march out like moths, without rest by day or night.

18. "Farad Khan's grandson, named Rustam-i-Zaman, who prides himself on great battles, shall be our general."

19–20. When he had said this, and honoured them all as befitted each, they bowed to their mighty lord, and all those captains of the army set out together from the city called Vijaya, roaring their war cries.

21. Then the spies whom Shiva had sent close to Adil came quickly to the hill of Pranala and reported that news.

22. Hearing that the king had taken that fine fort, Adil grieved like a serpent whose raised hood has been seized by Garuda.

23. Hearing that Shiva, the lord of the hill, had brought the hill under his control, Ali Shah was tormented in his heart with anxiety day after day.

24. Then, thinking himself not enough to subdue him, he quickly called for the army of the lord of Delhi.

25–27. King Shiva too, hearing that the enemy captains, firmly armoured, wished again to fight, placed a fierce army to guard the hill of Pranala and himself, full of zeal and loudly eager to conquer them, went swiftly forward with his armies, terrible as a forest fire with their hundreds of flashing weapons.

28. The enemy too, proud, advanced with their very formidable general, named Rustam, at their head.

29. Then Rustam, seeing the enemy army, very hard to withstand, said to Fazil and the other leaders of his troops:

*Rustam said:*

30. "Look at this enemy army, firmly arrayed, armed, full of zeal and steadiness, its banners raised.

31. "In it are great heroes, like the plough-bearer himself, who seem so many reflections of Shiva of matchless energy.

32. "This valiant one named Netaji, the leader of Shiva's army, great and proud, the best of those who bear arms, will fight us.

33. "Likewise the young Yadavrao, in armour, with a large army, is angry and coming to overpower us.

34. "Kharate, who crushes enemy strength, bold and strong, wants to conquer us together with his son named Hanumant.

35. "Pandhare, like a Pandava in battle, with his white banner, master and maker of a great army, will fight us wonderfully on the stage of battle.

36. "Hilal too, sword in hand, terrible as Death and as the flame at the end of time, will do great service to the Bhosle.

37–39. "Hiroji Ingale, a mass of splendour and strong-armed; Bhimaji Wagh; the excellent Siddhoji Pawar; the great hero named Godaji, born of the Jagtap family that sets the world right; and a Mahadik, like a second Parashurama; and other great captains of the army, masters of war, go forward ahead of King Shiva.

40. "King Shiva himself, the Bhosle, slayer of enemy kings, like an immortal, will surely carry the battle through.

41. "So let these leaders of our troops on every side marshal each his own army and stand at the head of the battle.

42. "I myself, a great fighter, shall guard the middle; let the valiant Fazil guard the left wing.

43. "Let Malik Itbar, with Sadat, guard the other wing of the vast army.

44. "Fateh Khan, Aziz Khan's son, of great fame, and Mulla Yahya, shall be busy guarding the rear.

45. "Santaji Ghorpade, Sarjerao Ghatge and other armed soldiers shall guard the army on every side."

46. Told this by him, they all took their places and, without distraction, guarded the whole army.

47. At that very moment King Shiva too, marshalling his own army, spoke to his warriors fitting words:

48. "Let Netaji, master of the army of four arms, attack Fazil; let Wagh, slayer of enemy heroes, go against Mulla Yahya.

49. "Let Ingale advance against Malik Itbar; let the proud Mahadik fight Fateh Khan.

50. "Let Siddhoji, born of the Pawar line, face Sadat; let Goda take on the Ghatge and the Ghorpade.

51. "And I shall strike down in battle the brave Rustam, the leader of the Muslim army, who stands at its forefront.

52. "Let Kharate and Pandhare attack on the right of the army, and let Hilal and the Yadava attack on the other side."

53. When the king of kings had spoken thus, those great fighters raised lion-roars together with the sound of the kettledrums.

54–55. Then the sound of kettledrums of many kinds, of trumpets, kahala horns, panava drums everywhere, gomukha drums and horns in the two armies filled the sky and shook the quarters.

56. Then the whole sky looked many-coloured with the splendour of the banners moving all around at the front.

57. Then the two armies, overflowing their bounds, roaring, very terrible, like the eastern and western oceans, met each other.

58. Then the leading fighters of both armies, their minds proud of their strength of arm, roaring in fury, their horses spurred on with eagerness, drenched the earth of battle with streams of blood.

59. Then the sky, filled with masses of dust raised by the hooves of the galloping horses, grew grey like a lake at the coming of the rains when the new water flows in.

60. As the clouds of the season of rain cover the sky with heavy showers, so the many running archers at once covered the space between earth and sky with masses of arrows.

61. A man who, seeing his enemy and delighted, drew his bow in battle — alas, it was a marvel that his hand, cut through by the arrow loosed from his enemy's hand, did not drop it.

62. On the field of battle, wanting to gather the flowers of great fame, heroes at once drew the creepers of their bows and, with the skill of their hands, quickly loosed swarms of arrows like bees upon the enemy.

63. Banner-bearers whose heads had been quickly cut off by the sharp swords of the advancing enemy warriors, falling to the ground and clasping it, did not let go of their banners, for their fists were clenched tight.

64. Then Shiva himself, with fury, attacked Rustam with many troops of horse in the front ranks, who rushed into the battle at speed, carrying great pride.

65. At once Shiva's fine warriors, drawing their bows and fighting with unerring arrows, angrily cut off the heads of the Muslims, great fighters as they were, who rushed at them.

66. The warriors in Rustam's army could not bear at all, in that great clash, the mass of weapons like thunderbolts hurled with zeal by the Bhosles' army.

67. Then the general of Shiva's army, mightier than all, attacked like the wind on every side Fazil, who stood in a fort made of a mass of elephants and roared loudly like a cloud, and threw him into disorder.

68. Seeing Netaji, leader of the Bhosle king's army, angry, Fazil at once loudly sang the prologue of a play called "Flight" — and acted it out at once.

69. Some young heroes adorned with jewelled ornaments, their bodies smeared red with blood, casting off fear of others, endowed with heroic glory, lay down on beds of arrows on the field of battle.

70. In the great battle some warriors were at once destroyed by Kharate, some by Pandhare, some by the Yadava himself, and some by Wagh himself as he attacked.

71. Hiroji, very fierce in deed, eager for battle, attacked with zeal and crushed the enemy; and the one named Hilal, his splendour dark with the smoke rising from the fire of his wrath, killed some.

72. When Rustam had thrown down his weapon and fled, and Fazil too had quickly slipped away from the battle, the troops led by Sadat, in disorder, found no protector of their own.

73. Then, as the broken Rustam, with five or six horsemen, slipped away from the field of battle, the whole of Ali Shah's army, utterly without refuge, quickly began to flee in all ten directions.

74. Then King Shiva did not seize Rustam, broken and fleeing in terror before him, even though he was near; for kings of the solar line do not show their valour against cowards.

75. "They who left us in battle and at once ran away of their own accord — alas, we are ashamed to take refuge with them again, shameless as they are." Thinking this in their hearts, as it were, the rut-maddened elephants of Adil's army sought the strong Shiva, the Bhosle, as their protector and refuge.

76. The glory of the battle wore an indescribable good fortune, with the splendour of the heaps of ornaments fallen from the limbs of enemies whom Shiva killed with zeal and joy, drawing the eyes of the people with wonder.

77. How did the column of headless trunks cross that river of blood — deep, its heart known only to hundreds of horses that sank in it and rose again with effort, roaring, terrible, of wondrous force, its banks at once filled with a flood of fear?

78. So the king, turning back Rustam and his men and taking that country into his own hands, delighted the lotus faces of the women of the quarters with the sweet sound of his kettledrums.

*Thus ends the twenty-fourth canto, called "The Rout of Rustam and the Others", in the Samhita of a hundred thousand verses, the Anupurana of the line of the Sun, brought to light by Kavindra Paramananda of Nidhivasa.*
