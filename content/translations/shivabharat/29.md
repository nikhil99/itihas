# Canto 29

*The prince of poets said:*

1–3. Then, when the god, lord of light, had reached the middle of the sky and, joining with Shiva's energy, set out to scorch them, the Raybagin, proud and spirited, seeing the whole army in that great forest — a forest never seen before, its paths held by the enemy, where no wind could be found — suffering and dejected in many ways, said to Kartalab:

*The Raybagin said:*

4. "You have done a shameful thing, bringing the army on your lap: you have rashly entered this forest, which is the shelter of the lion Shiva.

5. "Alas, by you, so very zealous, the army of the lord of Delhi brought here has been thrown into the lion's mouth.

6. "All the glory the lord of Indraprastha has won up to today, you have drowned in this forest.

7. "See — behind, before, and on the left and right sides, the enemies stand, eager to fight.

8. "All these soldiers of yours, with their well-aimed arrows, sit silent here, alas, like figures in a picture.

9. "Ah! Shaista Khan, the dull-witted general of the lord of Delhi, has thrown you, with your army, into the fire of the enemy's might.

10. "Your enemy wants to take you alive at once and lead you away; and you, shut in this forest like a blind man, wish to fight!

11. "Man's effort is justified only when it bears fruit; otherwise daring only serves to make one a laughing-stock.

12. "So go now, at once, to that king for refuge, and free yourself with your army from the noose of death."

13. Roused there in this way by the Raybagin, the great hero, bold as he was, gave up his eagerness for battle.

14. Then, taking a humble attitude, he sent to Shiva this message, which made his mind known:

15–25. Then, announced by the mace-bearers there, that envoy bowed his head and saw from afar the king, whose arms reached his knees: very handsome, with a tall neck and a broad chest; wearing a crest; bearing auspicious marks; of great body and great energy; two quivers fastened at his sides like wings; with fine jewelled ornaments, seated on his horse like Hari on Garuda; within an array of many horsemen bearing armour, bows, arrows and swords; his armour fastened on, impenetrable; splendid with a fine helmet; a bow hanging from his right shoulder; the sky lit by the bosses of his great shield worn on his back; adorned with a silken scarf hanging from a belt of gold; a tall spear-point fixed in his shining right hand; very gentle, yet very terrible with his own might; served properly by many soldiers placed where they belonged by the gold-staffed mace-bearers in front who cleared the way; fiercer than the fierce one, more unbearable than fire, more pitiless than Nairriti, stronger than the wind, richer than the lord of wealth, mightier than the wielder of the thunderbolt, crueller than the bearer of the rod, wiser in policy than Varuna, more delightful than the moon, more unconquerable than the god of love.

26. As that envoy of the copper-faced men came up with his head bowed, the king looked at him with his lotus eyes.

27. Then, at Shiva's bidding, given with a slight lift of his brow, the bearer of the message himself spoke words of measured length:

*The envoy said:*

28. "He whom people know as Kartalab, unconquerable like a second lord of Lanka, makes this request to you:

29. "'At the command of Shaista Khan, and as the occasion required, this country of yours has, alas, been seen by us.

30. "'This country of yours, guarded by you, has not come under anyone else's power, as a jewel long held by a serpent is its own.

31. "'Alas! In two or three days here I have found not even water, let alone life. So, by granting me safety, give me my life.

32. "'Having come to the foot of the Sahya mountain as if to the underworld, our minds have long been numbed, and our manhood forgotten.

33. "'This Sahya forest of yours is thicker even than Tungaranya. In this place like a great ocean, be our protector.

34. "'Your father, the great-minded King Shah, a great king — though I am a Muslim, he showed me boundless affection.

35. "'Forgetting all that, as another's servant, I knowingly treated you, who are like fire, with contempt.

36. "'So, giving up all I have, I wish to atone for this wrong of mine, strong-armed one, and I want to go away alive.

37. "'So permit me, O king, to leave this country. There is no one here like you, devoted to protecting those who take refuge.'"

38. When the envoy had spoken thus, truly, and fallen silent, the king looked at his own soldiers and answered him:

*King Shiva said:*

39. "If you take refuge, then leave this country of mine with your army. You have safety from us.

40. "Go quickly from here and tell these words of mine to that frightened Muslim, who asks for mercy."

41. Hearing these pleasing words spoken by King Shiva, the envoy reported them to his master, who was waiting.

42. When that envoy had brought back the promise of safety, Kartalab sent tribute to Shiva.

43. Mitrasen and the other kings, though still fighting, having obtained safety, each quickly sent his own tribute to Shiva.

44. Until the promise of safety arrived, the Muslim soldiers still fighting were plundered by Shiva's army and hemmed in on every side.

45. One man then gave his shield to another and was let go; one quickly got down from his horse and was freed from fear.

46. One, alas, wearing a garment red with blood, at once laid down his weapon, as if he had taken the vow of renunciation.

47. One, whose ear was being cut off by someone greedy for his earrings, at once gave up the pearl-and-gem ornament from his own neck.

48. Some, saying "We belong to King Shiva", freed themselves from horsemen.

49. One man, though his head had been cut off by a sword, quickly ran at his attacker and, wonderfully, made him his companion.

50–51. Meanwhile, at the command of the lord of the Sahya, mace-bearers with raised hands and loud voices, as if angry, went to the leaders of the armies stationed in the forest and stopped them on every side from fighting the enemy.

52. Then the copper-faced soldiers, having obtained safety, quickly came out of that forest, now without fear, as if out of an enemy's forest.

53–60. Then, as the copper-faced men went quickly back the way they had come; as his own armies roared and the instruments sounded; as many mace-bearers with staffs in hand went in front; as bards filled the sky with special cries of victory; as fine bards in fine clothes and fine ornaments loudly recited songs of his fame; as boundless chests of treasure were quickly brought and set down here and there by his own people; as the elephants and horses, let go in the forest by the fleeing enemies, were brought in by the soldiers; as many platters, cups and ewers of gold, dropped by the enemy out of fear of their loads, and many other vessels too, were heaped up like mountains everywhere by his own people — Shiva, who had crushed the circle of his enemies with the staff of his fierce arm, looked at Netaji, the general, and said:

*King Shiva said:*

61. "I am going to overrun the realm that lies under Adil. But you must stay right here to destroy the copper-faced men.

62. "Do not think that the enemies, just because they have turned back, will not come back again; these copper-faced men are proud.

63. "My effort, which aims at quickly taking back recompense for the hill of Pranala, will now at once bear fruit, without fail."

64. Having thus taken counsel with his general there, the wise and victorious one had the drum for the march of conquest beaten.

65. Then at dawn the lord of the people mounted his finest horse and adorned that road with the array of his armies.

66. Advancing step by step and seeing towns, villages, forts and forests abandoned by his enemies, he felt great satisfaction.

67. Then the king went to Dalbhyapura (Dabhol), bowed to the lord of Dalbhya, and first of all brought that country swiftly under his control.

68. Brought under his rule by Shiva, the country at once became free of the fear that came from contact with the Muslims.

69–70. Then the strong-armed Jasvant, lord of Palivan, remembering the help he had himself given Johar, and afraid of Shiva, who chastised the unruly, as he drew near, at once took refuge with the lord of Shringarpur.

71. The valiant Suryarao, ruler of Prabhavali, also protected him from Shiva, as one would protect one's own guilty self.

72. But Shiva did not think that deed of Suryarao and Jasvant ignoble, for both were dependent on others.

73–74. Then the king, having placed at Dalbhyapura a fit officer, a hero ready for war, commander of two thousand, and gladdening with the gift of safety the people who asked for it, went on and in three or four days saw the town of Chiplun with its sandy banks.

75. There he gazed on the giver of boons, famed throughout the world, the long-lived son of Renuka, whose deeds are worthy of praise.

76. Then, full of devotion, he worshipped with great offerings that Parashurama, attended by his two brothers, Time and Desire.

77. Parashurama too, destroyer of the power of the lords of the earth who hated him, showed that ruler of the earth great favour.

78. There, in the holy field of Bhargava, the generous and compassionate king quickly delighted the hosts of Brahmins with the wealth he gave.

79–80. Then, seeing the town called Sangameshvar, so named because it lies at a confluence — abandoned that very moment by the Muslim officials, come under his own rule, rich in Brahmins and gods — that sage among kings went there, as to a dwelling place of divine sages.

81–82. Then, at his command, the Brahmin, son of King Nilakantha, at the head of a great force of foot soldiers, together with the warrior Tanaji, born of the Malusare line, his mind eager for the coming of the enemy, came to Sangameshvar.

83–84. "Stay at Sangameshvar to guard this country; and keep a careful watch on my armies at Shringarpur until I come. Give up enmity, and do as I have told you."

85. Having said this, the king then sent this message by a trusty envoy to the lord of Prabhavali.

86–88. Then, bringing at once under his own command that country — fragrant mostly with the blossoms of punnaga and bakula, its trees mostly betel vine, coconut and areca, its soil mostly sacred to the gods, its people mostly Brahmins, its hills mostly gardens, its rivers mostly holy places — this best of kings conquered Rajapur and shone.

89. Having killed the Muslim army, brought that region under the vast power of his own arm and at once given safety to all who sought his refuge, Shiva, king of kings, shone the more in Rajapur, beside which the sea bears the wealth of merchants in their ships.

*Thus ends the twenty-ninth canto, called "The Entry into His Own City", in the Samhita of a hundred thousand verses, the Anupurana of the line of the Sun, brought to light by Kavindra Paramananda.*
