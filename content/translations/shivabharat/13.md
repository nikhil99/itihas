# Canto 13

*The learned men said:*

1. Hearing that his father had been captured by the enemy, what did King Shambhu do, and what did King Shiva do?

2. And after capturing the great king, what did Mustafa, the commander of the army, do, and the evil-minded, unrighteous Mahmud?

*The prince of poets said:*

3. Shambhu, living in the city of Bengrul, was furious with Mustafa when he heard that his father had been captured by the enemy.

4. The valiant King Shiva too, hearing of this plight of King Shah, vowed to pay Adil back for the wrong.

5–6. The very proud Mustafa, wanting to take Bengrul, quickly ordered King Tanaji, leader of the Duryas, Vitthal Gopal, a Brahmin living by the warrior's trade, and the bold Farad Khan to set out at once.

7. Then the shrewd Mahmud too ordered captains of his army to go to Shiva's country.

8–12. Then the general named Fateh Khan, a great-minded man; Mina and Ratan, the two Shaikhs; the wrathful Fateh Khan; the cruel Sharfa Shah, a bowman rich in fame — these Muslims, armoured and equipped with weapons; Ghantika Mattaraj, his arrows like thunderbolts; the strong Baji Nayak, lord of Phaltan; and hundreds of other vassals, with gold-backed bows, golden armour, golden clothes and golden banners, carrying shields that shone with golden moons and bosses — these quickly attacked and took by force the town of Bilvasara.

13–14. Likewise the son of King Haibat, named Ballal, sure-handed, skilled and cruel in deeds, like a second Ashvatthama, surrounded by many troops tight-packed like lions, reached the town of Shirval without being checked by Shiva's armies.

15–16. Then, hearing of their coming, Shiva, staying on the summit of Purandar and like Karttikeya, son of the slayer of Pura, smiling, armoured, bow and arrows in hand, fully equipped, spoke these words to his steady warriors, equal to the plough-bearer:

*Shiva said:*

17. "My father the great king, rich in his own riches, trusted Mustafa Khan, and alas, he has met with misfortune.

18. "Do not trust the untrustworthy, and do not even trust the trusted, for fear born of trust cuts away even the roots.

19. "Though he had heard this saying of Vyasa, alas, the lord of the earth put his trust in that man, who was most untrustworthy.

20. "The vilest of Muslims, Mustafa, whose intentions and movements cannot be read, at the command of Adil Shah captured the great king.

21. "Even King Rama, son of Dasharatha, urged on by his doe-eyed wife, trusted the golden deer and was deceived by the ten-necked one.

22. "Yayati, son of Nahusha, too trusting, was deceived by Indra and quickly fell from heaven.

23. "Karna, who wore the armour he was born with, trusted the enemy of Bala, was stripped of it, and so was killed by Partha.

24. "Even Dharmaraja, foremost of the Bharatas, trusting, was beaten at dice by Shakuni, the helper of Suyodhana.

25. "So a discerning man, one who has properly studied the duties of kings with their marks, should never trust enemies.

26. "Embracing a deadly serpent, drinking halahala poison, and trusting enemies are reckoned the same.

27. "Fondling the young of monkeys, handling a black cobra, and keeping the friendship of the wicked lead equally to harm.

28–30. "As lighting a lamp on the porch of a blind man's house, building a dam of sand in a stream, threading broken pearls back together, splitting the stem of a plantain, as the world says, digging the sky or beating the water lead only to toil, so does serving the wicked.

31. "What did he do, who did not remember the science of policy, that teaches not to trust an enemy? He made himself a bed of burning khadira coals.

32. "If the thirst of the thirsty could be quenched by a mirage, then all good fortune could come from serving the wicked.

33. "The wicked man who looks for weak spots and vies with others should be known as more harmful than a snake; he should not be ignored.

34. "My great king guarded the whole kingdom of this Mahmud and kept his word. What has he done to him in return?

35. "He has become the enemy of those who did him good. Though his fortune makes him honoured, it is a wonder that this Adil Shah, now without support, does not fall.

36. "My elder brother, who stays in the city of Bengrul and is besieged there by Farad Khan and other enemies, will fight there.

37. "And I, guarding these mountain forts without fail, will fight here with the enemy armies, fully prepared.

38. "I here myself, and the valiant King Shambhu there — both of us fighting, we shall set our father free.

39. "Then, when we have humbled him, the very arrogant Mahmud will release the great king, and his own pride with him.

40. "If Mahmud does not free our honoured father, together with his own dharma, he will reap the fruit of his own deed.

41. "If in his folly Adil harms the great king, my father will at once destroy him together with his helpers.

42. "Prison and other bonds are not enough to bind those whose minds are always bound by the cords of dharma.

43. "Long ago I took the famous Jayavalli and set up there Chandraraj, who coveted it.

44. "The fearsome Ghorpades, like angry serpents, have fallen quite quiet on seeing me, the snake-charmer.

45. "Before, I set out at once to fight, put the lord of Phaltan to flight, captured him alive and let him go.

46. "Now these warriors, Fateh Khan and the rest, gathered together, will meet us and fight like maddened elephants.

47. "This strong Ballal, guarding a strong force, has taken Shirval and thinks himself very great.

48. "So go there quickly from here, capture that very strong man, and free Shirval today.

49. "Then tomorrow, or the day after, here or there, we shall see the mighty Fateh Khan and fight him."

*The prince of poets said:*

50. Hearing Shiva's words, those soldiers by the thousand filled the sky with mighty lion-roars.

51–56. Then Godaji, crusher of enemies, born of the Jagtap family, a great fighter who delighted in battle; Bhimaji of the Wagh line, terrible as a second Bhima; Kante, who broke the pride of enemies and the pride of the arrogant; Shivaji, born of the Ingale family, the very ornament of battle, the tip of his lance terrible, like Death; Bhikaji, born of the Chor line, who stole the fortune of enemy heroes, the best of the band, fearless, with a very terrible army, in the work of war; and Bhairav his brother, terrible as Bhairava in battle, who struck terror into enemy hearts — these brave men of the lord of the mountain fort, bright as the sun, splendid each in his own glory, bowed to him and set out.

57. Over all of them Shiva appointed Kavaji as commander, as Vasudeva appointed Satyaki over all the Vrishni heroes.

58–59. Armoured, roaring like clouds, riding horses in full harness, they came down from Purandar, passed the night there, and in the morning, eager to conquer the enemy, turned towards the march and had the drums beaten.

60–61. Then, as if splitting the surface of the earth with the feet of their foot soldiers and as if cutting the circle of the sky with their whirling horses, as if scattering the fire of the world's end upon their enemies, those heroes quickly saw Shirval.

62. But seeing Shiva's strong army approach, the enemy said to his own very wise foot soldiers:

*Ballal said:*

63. "Do not be afraid at the sight of this very proud enemy army. In battle, death is best; flight is the worst.

64. "At Fateh Khan's command we have come to Shirval. Our position here is fixed, like the fixed star.

65. "If you are afraid, then at my command stay here and rely on me as on a second rampart.

66. "Our master's business will not be accomplished by our dying alone. So for the great battle let us take refuge in the rampart."

67. "In this very hard time that fate has brought, even this little fort, the one fate has shown us, will bring success in the fight.

68. "Proud men, fighting with pride in this Shirval, we will give our heads here, but in battle we will not give up our fame.

69–73. "For fame the ten-headed one's enemy gave up his beloved wife; for fame Bali, lord of the demons, went to the underworld; for fame the enemy of the demons took on the body of a tortoise; for fame Shibi cut off his own flesh and gave it; for fame the axe-bearing Khanda drank the halahala at once; for fame Dadhichi cut out his own bones and gave them away; for fame Kashyapi gave away the whole earth; for fame Svarnadi's son lay on a bed of arrows. So today, for fame and for gain, we will fight our enemies until they strike us down, picking out the best of us one by one."

74. When Ballal had spoken thus, his warriors by the thousand at once took refuge in the fort and roared like elephants.

75. Then Kavaji, eager for battle, seeing the enemy warriors take refuge in the rampart, said this to his troops:

*Kavaji said:*

76. "Ah! This strong Ballal, who came to Shiva's country wanting to conquer it, is sheltering behind a rampart.

77. "Taking refuge in this Shirval of ours, ah, the dull-witted man shows the measure of his own wit.

78. "It has no bastions, no moat as big as its name suggests. So, soldiers, do not think this fort hard to take.

79. "Surround it; block every road around it; and in a single moment fill the whole ditch.

80. "Make your horses leap like birds over it, or break it open with pickaxes.

81. "Dig it up; quickly dig out its foundations. Is this fort Lanka, that we should fear it?

82. Told this loudly by Kavaji, the soldiers, roused to battle, at once surrounded that fort on every side and began the assault.

83. Seeing these before them, of a valour beyond the gods, the bowmen inside took the start of battle for a festival and began to shoot.

84. Whoever raised his head above to look at them had it cut off at once and became like the headless planet Ketu.

85. As lines of bees fly from the branches of trees, sharp arrows came out from the bows of the heroes.

86. Fierce, very strong men, drawing their bows to the ear, cut off the heads of heroes with sharp arrows.

87. Arrows loosed by sure hands fell and went so deep into the ground that the lord of serpents below saw them.

88–89. The men on the rampart showered the enemy heroes with wheels, iron bars, pestles, mortars, stones, millstones, burning firebrands, heaps of khadira coals, boiling oil and many kinds of weapons.

90. Though struck by the enemy, Shiva's troops, raising their weapons, surrounded the fort and looked all the finer for it.

91. Then some soldiers in their fury broke into the fort in many places with long maces and iron bars.

92. Some made holes in it with lance-thrusts; some climbed up ladders and attacked the wall.

93. Some, mounted on horses swift as Garuda, looking all around, tried hard to leap over it.

94. And Kavaji smashed the gateway with heavy blows of maces and other weapons.

95. When the hero burst through the gateway and entered the enclosure, Adil's army came at him and fought there.

96. The strong Ballal, meeting that onrush like the submarine fire, shone as he took the attack and struck back.

97–98. Very handsome, tall, armour fastened on, young, carrying lance and bow, steady, surrounded by his soldiers, riding a tall horse, he shone the more, they say, like a lamp at the moment it goes out.

99–100. Then Bhika and Bhima, Tuka, Goda and Sada, Sambha and other fine warriors, with Kavaji at their head, their tall horses leaping quickly and weapons in their hands, struck Ballal and his men with fierce anger.

101. Then, in that blinding darkness of hatred, the weapons of the fighters on both sides clashed against one another.

102–103. As the riders of horses swifter than Garuda rushed at one another in the fury of battle, weapons struck weapons by the hundred, like flashes of lightning striking flashes of lightning in the sky.

104. Lance-bearer struck lance-bearer, bowman bowman, mace-man mace-man, and was then himself cut to pieces.

105. Shield-bearer fought only shield-bearer, armoured man only armoured man, bowman only bowman in that battle.

106. Arrows that could not pierce the armour and flew quickly upward shone for a moment in the sky like the rays of the sun.

107. When arrows pierced the armour of warriors and entered the earth, streams of blood began to flow one after another.

108. There the headless trunks of men whose heads had been cut off by arrows from angry hands spurted blood and bellowed.

109. Then the trunks of elephants and the necks of horses, cut through by arrows, fell to the ground.

110. Hosts of heroes bearing bows scattered many enemy heads, cut off by arrows, all over the field of battle.

111. One man rushed at another who came at him mace in hand and seized that crocodile of the ocean of battle.

112. One who had lost his own hand there, cut off, attacked enemies like an elephant with his one remaining hand.

113–114. In a moment the Ingales killed twenty-five warriors there, the Poles five and seven, the Chors fourteen, the Ghatges nine and eight, the Waghs sixteen, and Kavaji killed nineteen fine warriors.

115. Then a river of blood flowed swiftly there, rising from the bodies of many foot soldiers, horses and elephants killed in the fight.

116. When the enemy heroes had beaten it and surrounded it by force, Ballal's army fled the battle in fear.

117. Haibat's son could not steady his army there, turned back by the enemy and wavering.

118. Then, they say, full of rivalry, raising his weapon, he charged the enemy swiftly in anger, as Vritra charged the immortals.

119. As many arrows as were in his quiver, so many foremost men of Kavaji's did he bring down.

120. Just as he raised his lance to frighten the enemies around him, Kavaji struck him down with a blow of his lance.

121–122. When Haibat's son, fighting with pride, had been felled by the commander of Shiva's army as a lion fells a maddened elephant, and the ground was smeared with blood, fat, marrow and flesh, no one in his army could keep steady.

123. Then the proud Kavaji let go free the hundreds of people who sought refuge, holding grass in their teeth.

124. Some, fighting in the pride of battle, rushed forward, were cut to pieces by arrows and went to Shatakratu's realm.

125–126. Some with feet cut off, some with hands cut off, some with armour cut through, some with chests cut open, some with hips broken, some with elbows broken, rolled on the ground crying piteously and fell senseless.

127–129. Then, while the enemy lay on the field of battle, the fine warriors Kavaji and the others gladly took his elephants, tall horses, ornaments of many kinds, bright clothes, armour, weapons, litters, treasure and other equipment, and went to the hill of Purandar to see their lord, who was like Purandara.

130. Having killed the enemy heroes and swiftly finished their task, Kavaji and the other soldiers bowed with bent heads before the son of Shah, presenting the many fine horses taken from the enemy heroes, a wealth of gold, strings of pearls and jewels.

*Thus ends the thirteenth canto in the Samhita, the Anupurana brought to light by Kavindra Paramananda.*
