# Canto 4

*The prince of poets said:*

1–3. Then the Nizam, the master who sided with Khelkarna and the other sons of King Vithoji, men of wonderful deeds, knowing that the lord of the Yadavas — who had a large following, a very strong army and many troops, who destroyed his opponents like a second wielder of the thunderbolt — was very hard to restrain, plotted a great deceit in his heart.

4. Learning of his wicked plan, that strong-armed leader of the Yadavas left and joined the lord of Delhi.

5. When the lord of the Yadavas left the Nizam's country and went away, Adil Shah at once saw his chance and was glad.

6. For he had long been humbled by the Nizam at every step. So he himself made a treaty with the lord of the copper-faced men.

7. The noble and valiant lord of Delhi, a long-time rival of the Nizam, at once promised to do what Adil Shah wanted.

8. Jahangir, lord of the copper-faced men, of very fierce valour, sent an army to help Ibrahim.

9. Gaining that army of copper-faced men, Adil Shah thought his enemy the Nizam no more than a straw.

10–21. The Nizam was guarded on every side by King Shah; by Sharif the bowman; by the hero Khelkarna of praiseworthy virtues; by the strong King Malaji and Mambaji; by Nagoji, strong as a king of elephants; by Parsoji and King Tryambak; by Kakaji, famous in battle for the strength of his arm; by Hambirrao Chavan, conqueror of enemies; by Mudhoji, lord of Phaltan; by Narsimharao and many other warriors of the Nishada and other tribes; by Ballal Tripade and others; by the mighty Vithoji Kante; by the famous Mambaji, son of Dattaji Jagannath; by the Brahmin Narsimha, called Pingale; by Sundar, son of Jagdev; by Yakut Khan, the proud charioteer; by the brave, handsome and fierce Mansur; by the proud Johar Khan and Hamid; by the hero Atash, fiery as a flame; by the Abyssinian Malik Ambar, valiant as the sun; by his proud son Fateh Khan, swift and fierce; by the renowned sons of Adam Khan; and by other great generals besides. Proud of his own arm's strength, destroyer of hosts of enemies, blazing like fire, the Nizam cared nothing for his enemies.

22–27. Adil Shah, relying on the help of the lord of Delhi, himself made ready to fight the Nizam Shah. Jalal and Jahan, Khanjir and Sikandar, Karmulla, Khelil, Sujan and Samad — all these valiant Muslims came with Bahadur. And the king named Duda, devoted to war; Udaram, a famous Brahmin given to the warrior's work; Dadaji Vishvanath, like Bharadvaja in battle; Raghav, Achal, Jasvant and Bahadur — the strong-armed sons of Jadhavrao — together with all the generals, came to the South with the lord of Delhi's permission.

28. Those valiant men, unchecked and swift as winds sweeping across the sky, overran the Nizam's country.

29–32. Mustafa, Masud, Farad and Dilavar; Sarja, Yakut, Khairat and Ambar; Ankush — these and other Muslims of matchless valour, many friends and dependants of Ibrahim; a Brahmin named Dhundhi and Rustam of the same caste; and many Maharashtra kings, Ghatge and others — these too, captains of Adil's army, came in due order, putting the mighty Mulla Muhammad at their head.

33. Then, as they came from the north and the south, Ambar, sent by the Nizam, went out fearlessly to meet them.

34. There King Shah and the other kings gathered round him, as the gods gathered round Mahasena in the war with the demon Taraka.

35. Then there was a dreadful battle between Ambar and his enemies, which brought joy to the ghouls, ghosts, vampires and night-stalkers.

36. As the dust whirled up by the hooves of running horses darkened the sky, the fierce-rayed sun seemed to wear a covering of thick clouds.

37. A line of dust from the earth, grazing the clouds, shone like a stairway for heroes about to climb at once to heaven.

38–41. The sky was filled with the neighing of horses, the trumpeting of elephants, the lion-roars of warriors, the booming of drums, the loud twang of strung bows, the rustle of countless fluttering banners, and the songs of praise of deep-voiced bards. The earth was split a hundred ways by the heavy tread of warriors with weapons in their hands as they rushed at one another.

42. Alas! The heads of fighters were cut off in a moment by the sharp arrows of their opponents, which fell like the touch of Death.

43. The heads of heroes fell to the ground there, their hair wet with blood, their eyes red, their teeth biting their lips.

44. The swords of warriors, gripped in tight fists, fell upon the tusks of elephants, hard as diamond.

45. With his sword one fighter cut an opponent in two at a stroke, and a moment later his own headless body fell.

46. The temples of elephants, pierced by hundreds of arrows, shone as they poured out streams of blood along with their rut-fluid.

47. Great heroes lay as if weary in a great sleep on the banks of rivers whose waves were the blood of men, horses and elephants.

48. Their riders struck down by heroes with lances in hand, horses, greatly excited and terrified, ran about everywhere.

49–51. Then Shah and Sharif, the mighty Khelaji, the black-faced Muslims who did Ambar's pleasure, and the great and valiant heroes King Hambir and the rest, with razor-arrows, discs, swords, lances and broadswords in their hands, cut down the great army of copper-faced men from end to end. Frantic with fear and wanting to live, it took to every one of the ten directions.

52. Seeing that army of copper-faced men fleeing, Adil Shah's army too began to scatter.

53. Then Manuchehar, a copper-faced man proud of the strength of his rut-maddened elephants, himself guarded the rear of that fleeing army.

54–55. Seeing him standing firm, proud and fierce, blocking their path to victory like a second Vindhya across the road ahead, all the Bhosles, Shah, Sharif and the rest, strong-armed and of great valour, began to strike.

56. Those armoured men fought that very proud man, who stood within a rampart of elephants like great mountains.

57. Then Sharif, unshaken, furious in battle, wheeling his spear, struck down the array of elephants, his mind unconfused.

58. Brave fighters with tridents, arrows, bows, maces and iron clubs, riding elephants, blocked the steady Sharif as he advanced.

59. As that proud man fought on in fury, they brought Sharif down with sharp arrows from every side.

60–61. When his younger brother, a hero who had shattered the enemy's elephants, was pierced by enemy arrows and went the way of heroes, Shah in fury, surrounded by Khelkarna and his other brothers, swiftly charged Manuchehar and his army.

62. Then, his rut-maddened elephants driven back in terror by fine spears, the valiant copper-faced man turned and went back.

63. As he withdrew from the battle with his elephants unharmed, the Nizam's soldiers raised lion-roars.

64. Then the Nizam's enemies fled at speed, some making for the north, some for the west, some for the east.

65. Then King Shah and the other kings, glad at heart, pursued the fleeing men and took them all prisoner by force.

66. Many heroes, copper-faced men and others, fierce in battle, they brought from the battlefield with their arms bound tight and quickly set them before Ambar.

67. Having thus conquered his enemies with the strength of the Bhosles' arms to rely on, the valiant Ambar, amid the roar of drums mixed with cries of victory, quickly went to see the Nizam.

68. By the glory of the Bhosles he had swiftly crushed the army of the lord of Delhi, rich in strange splendour and unconquerable by others, and Adil Shah's army of matchless strength; and he had captured in battle the fierce captains of the army, proud as they were. Then the general Ambar, with the Bhosles, bowed before the Nizam.

*Thus ends the fourth canto, called "The Triumph of the Nizam", in the Samhita of a hundred thousand verses, the Anupurana of the line of the Sun, brought to light by Kavindra Paramananda of Nidhivasa.*
