# Canto 3

*The prince of poets said:*

1–2. Then, when King Vithoji, his father's brother, had also gone to heaven, the wise, steady and very brave King Shah, who followed the rules of kingship and obeyed his mother's word, took the great burden of the kingdom on his own arm.

3–4. Sambhaji, Khelaji, Malaji, Mambaji, Nagoji, Parsoji, Tryambakji and Vakaji: these brothers born of one womb were the sons of King Vithoji, equal in valour to Indra. Shah and Sharif were the two mighty sons of King Maloji.

5–7. These were eager to conquer lands, able in the work of war, doers of the Nizam's pleasure, with bows always in their hands, with bodies tall as mountains, bright as the thousand-rayed sun, made matchless in energy by the great strength of their arms, their large following, their army and other qualities. Following the counsel of Malik Ambar, they cared nothing for their enemies.

8–9. One day the Nizam Shah came out of the inner palace with his trusted servants and ministers and sat on the throne in the hall of audience. Jadhavrao and all the others saw him, bowed in due order, and then quickly set off, each to his own lodging.

10. As they left, those men who vied with one another in spirit, there was a great crush of people outside the gateway of the hall.

11–14. There, with mace-bearers in front loudly clearing people from the way, went the kings: thick-shouldered and broad-chested, armed, wearing crowns and earrings, their bodies covered in coats of mail, bearing great necklaces of pearls and gems and armlets, of fine marks, lotus-eyed lords of the earth, each surrounded by his own troops, with tall banners carried before him. Some rode horses, some elephants, some went in litters, all of unchecked power, in that crowded place.

15. Then an elephant at the head of a king named Khandagale broke loose by force and charged, trampling the troops of others.

16. Though its driver checked it at every step with blows of the goad, that elephant wrought great slaughter among the soldiers.

17. No one could stop that fearless beast as it crushed whole troops and roared like a cloud at the end of the world.

18. Then Datta and the other sons of Jadhavrao could not bear its roaring, as one elephant cannot bear the roar of a rival.

19. Then, at Datta's command, warriors struck that rut-maddened elephant with arrows, swords, lances, javelins and spears.

20–21. Its armour torn by many warriors, that elephant of terrible deeds suddenly scattered the horsemen with its great trunk. Some it seized with its trunk and hurled away, some it threw down, and some it knocked down and crushed under its feet.

22. Seeing the rout of his own troops, Dattaji went for that elephant like a lion fixing its eyes on its prey.

23. Then that elephant, maddened by the weapons that had struck it hard, shook its head and trumpeted at the head of the battle.

24. Then Sambhaji and Khelaji, the two sons of King Vithoji, came up to help Khandagale.

25. Those two guarded the elephant, which had now come under Datta's power, its body smeared with blood, like a mountain streaked with red ochre.

26. Then King Datta left that elephant with its broken trunk and attacked the strong-armed King Sambhaji.

27–29. As those two fought a duel in anger, a great crowd of warriors came running, and then hand-to-hand fighting broke out between the two forces. As Datta charged Sambhaji, who shone like Indra, the lord King Shah, Sambhaji's brother, ignoring even his ties by marriage to the Yadavas, guarded Sambhaji's side.

30. Then the valiant King Datta, shield in hand, whirled the tip of his sword around him and made a circle as if to fence himself in.

31. In that sudden battle, the quarters were deafened by the shouts of the warriors who rushed in and the noise of slapped arms.

32. Then that hero, making circles with the tip of his sword, danced on the stage of battle, as it were, in the greatest style.

33–37. The crowned heads of the best warriors rolled on the ground; arms with their bows were cut through by swords; armour was split by blades, arrows, axes and lances; hands with their arrows were cut off by sharp discs; the dust of the battlefield was laid by streams of blood flowing from horses, rut-maddened elephants and their riders; the battlefield was covered with the cloth of banners; men fell with bodies full of arrows. Then that steady Yadava hero, slayer of enemies, meeting King Sambhaji, pierced the orb of the sun — he fell and went to the world of the sun.

38–39. When the lord of the Yadavas, who had been going on ahead, heard that his own son Datta, matchless in deeds, had been killed by Sambhaji in battle, he turned back, his eyes red with anger, intent on killing King Sambhaji.

40. When the lord of the Yadavas was seized by that rage, the earth with its mountains, forests and islands shook.

41. "The wretch who killed my son, dearer to me than life itself — I will kill him and have my wish."

42. Seeing his father-in-law, brave as a god, seized by anger in this way, King Shah, wishing to protect his own side, fought him.

43. Seeing his son-in-law fighting, the bold enemy-tamer struck Shah on the arm, which was like the king of serpents.

44. Struck by that sword, Shah fell into a deep faint, and only by his own steadiness did he somehow keep his life.

45–47. Then, after overcoming Khelaji, humbling other kings and defeating the Nizam's army of black-faced men, that Yadava, firm of grip, raised his sword and in fury rushed at Sambhaji, unconquerable in battle. Then Sambhaji, serene as if he were laughing outright, quickly put his hand to the hilt of his own sword.

48. Then a battle broke out between those two as they vied with each other, like two maddened elephants, which filled the hearts of others with fear.

49. Then that Yadava, conqueror of enemies, after bearing many sword blows from Sambhaji, laid him on the ground with his own sword.

50. Laying low on the ground the man who had killed his son, that Yadava hero took his revenge.

51–52. Alas! When the great King Jadhavrao had laid low in battle the eldest son of King Vithoji, the Nizam's whole army could do nothing to answer it and lost heart.

53–54. Then, when their master the Nizam had soothed them and checked them, the two armies, deeply troubled, drew back from their quarrel. Lifting from the battlefield the bodies of Sambhaji and Datta, and grieving, each went in despair to its own camp.

55. Then all of Sambhaji's younger brothers, Khelkarna and the rest, sick at heart, mourned their eldest brother.

56. Jadhavrao had the funeral rites performed for his son, and Khelkarna duly performed them for his eldest brother.

57. When the Yadava, wise as he was, reflected in his proud heart on this battle, so contrary to their close ties by marriage, fought with rivals of the Bhosle line, we think he felt some remorse.

*Thus ends the third canto, called "The Sudden Clash", in the Anupurana of the line of the Sun, brought to light by Kavindra Paramananda of Nidhivasa.*
