# Canto 2

*The prince of poets said:*

1–2. When Sharif's elder brother reached his fifth year, the great and noble King Maloji, taking up the bow he loved, set out with a great army for a campaign against Indrapura, sent by the Nizam.

3–5. There, as he fought for his master and was attacked from every side by many strong warriors armed with clubs who struck at him again and again, he set flowing a great, swift river of blood that carried along foot soldiers, maddened elephants and horses. Then, enraged like Death, blazing with his own fierce energy, he sent the enemy warriors before him and set out himself on the road to heaven.

6. Hearing that news, terrible as a thunderbolt, the virtuous Uma fell to the ground like a plantain tree struck by the wind.

7. Bereft as the beauty of day is bereft of the sun, she sank by force, without support, into an ocean of darkness.

8–9. Then King Vithoji, his eyes filled with tears, wishing to calm his brother's wife, who was so stricken, overwhelmed by grief for her husband and crying like an osprey, himself spoke to her in a voice choked with sobs, great-minded as he was:

10. "Know that this world is always passing, noble lady, and give up your grief. Your husband has left his own people and gone to heaven.

11. "This is the road heroes pray for, the heroes who never turn back: death in the forefront of battle from the blows of enemy weapons.

12. "Those who want at once to drink nectar in the world of the gods, who look to their own goal, surely have no love for their own kin.

13. "Alas! Your husband has abandoned you, virtuous lady, and your little children, and gone to the other world. His heart was hard.

14. "Without doubt the bodies of the embodied are perishable. Even when guarded with care, they waste away when life runs out.

15–17. "This body — anointed with fine unguents, decked with fine ornaments, dressed in soft and lovely clothes, fed on many kinds of delicacies to be eaten, licked, drunk and sucked, cared for with light, sweet and wholesome food, fragrant with the smoke of black aloewood incense, living in delightful mansions lit by bright jewelled lamps —

18–20. "— laid to sleep on a lovely bed soft as shirisha flowers, fanned by fans cooled with spray, its feet pressed again and again by the lotus hands of beautiful women, kept alive with every effort and many remedies: this body, as if ungrateful, follows no one into the other world. So in this world no one truly belongs to anyone.

21–22. "Loving wives, sons who bear good marks, mother and father, brothers born of one womb, friends and enemies, fortune and misfortune — these last only as long as the body lasts.

23. "Here, O queen, are your two sons, Shah and Sharif. Full of energy, they shine like the sun and moon risen together.

24. "Through these two jewels of the family your fame will be bright. In all the world you will be called the mother of heroes.

25. "These two children, alas, still drinking milk, depend on you for their lives. Without you, how could they stay in this world even for a moment?

26. "So hold on to your life for the sake of these two lives, and take courage now at these words of mine."

27. Thus King Vithoji consoled his brother's wife, for the sake of the family's survival and above all of the kingdom's.

28. Turning her eyes, in her love, on her two sons, that sinless queen put aside her grief and held on to life.

29. Mastering even that great grief, the great-minded man performed for his brother all the rites for the other world.

30. Then the valiant King Vithoji, putting Shah and Sharif forward, made the whole kingdom secure.

31. When the Nizam heard of Maloji's death, he felt that his army was like a bird whose wings had been cut.

32–33. Then the great-minded Nizam summoned Maloji's two sons, Shah and Sharif, together with King Vithoji, consoled them, himself gave them their father's estate, and honoured them with lovely golden ornaments and clothes.

34. Pleased, the lord of Dharagiri also honoured them with priceless strings of jewels, elephants and horses, and sent the two of them home.

35. Brought up by their father's brother, the two boys' bodies, still in childhood, took on the highest splendour.

36. Then Sharif's elder brother, a home of every fortune and endowed with excellent marks, came to bear the title Raja.

37. All the great lords, though he was still a boy, honoured that strong-armed son of Uma and bowed to him.

38. As youth entered Shah's body, childhood shrank away little by little, as if crowded out.

39. His face, with the line of a moustache just appearing, was as beautiful as a young elephant's brow marked with the first streak of rut.

40–41. His face, golden and shining, with eyes stretched to the ears and a nose that frightened the parrots, was lovely and put the moon to shame. His arms reached to his knees and his body was wonderful.

42–44. When King Jadhavrao saw Maloji's son Shah — great in generosity, great in compassion, great in war, of great energy, marked with excellent signs — then, on a day fixed by astrologers and marked by fine omens, he gave him his daughter Jija, a jewel of a girl in a noble house, lotus-eyed, a maiden of good family, of auspicious marks and marked for victory.

45. Winning that great-souled man, she who had a radiant body and was deep in virtue shone like the Ganga reaching the ocean.

46–49. And he, winning that beloved wife, like the lotus-handed goddess of earth's fortune — rich in beauty and splendour, of noble descent; her smooth dark curls falling to her hips; her brows like the bow of the god of love, set on the half-moon of her forehead; long-eyed; her ears like golden shells; her nose straight; her teeth like fresh jasmine buds; her lips red; her face a full-blown lotus; her throat like a conch; her voice like the cuckoo's; her arms soft as lotus stalks; in the bloom of new youth —
50–59. — her hands like opening lotuses, her fingers playing like young shoots, her nails smooth and red, her navel deep, her waist slender, her thighs lovely as plantain stems, her ankles hidden, a famous lady; her feet marked with the signs of royalty, a home of every fortune; her smooth, glossy braid bound with many ornaments; the parting of her hair lined with rows of pearls and gems, her head bright with a red flower; golden earrings strung with pearls at their edges; a jewelled ornament on her forehead; lovely with a necklace of great pearls and gems, bearing clusters of jewels and pearls; armlets of jewels and gold, bracelets set with gems; her slender waist girdled with a jewelled belt; walking like a swan, with jingling jewelled anklets; her toes adorned with priceless jewelled rings; dressed in cloth of gold thread; in a bodice glittering with rows of pearls and gems — gazing at her bright face, himself bright-faced, that jewel of the solar line entered his home to the sound of music.

60. As she came in with her husband, humble and dependent on him, she seemed like Shri entering the house with Narayana.

61. The fortunate women of the town quickly gathered, gazing at her in wonder, and waved lamps before her.

62. As she bowed, her mother-in-law greeted her with blessings that never fail and looked at her daughter-in-law with delight.

63–64. The virtuous women praised her: devoted to serving her mother-in-law, dearer to her husband than anything, skilled in guarding her good name, modesty as if in bodily form, keeping to the bounds set by virtuous women, honoured in both families, bringing joy to her household.

65. Sharif too, strong-armed, took as his wife Durga, the virtuous daughter of King Vishvasrao.

66. Served by that slender daughter-in-law and by Jija, and with her two well-mannered sons, Uma found the highest joy.

67. Those two brothers, of countless virtues, shining with two wives of boundless virtue, at once made the whole earth bright with their glory and made their own mother rejoice.

68. With his brother, whose course was never checked, like Agni with the wind; fiercer in deed than Bhishma; victorious in battle after battle — the famous King Shah, of matchless strength, bow and arrows in hand, became, to do the Nizam's pleasure always, like Prithu, the ruler of the earth among all the kings.

*Thus ends the second canto, called "The Marriages of Shah and Sharif", in the Samhita of a hundred thousand verses, the Anupurana of the line of the Sun, brought to light by Paramananda of Nidhivasa.*
