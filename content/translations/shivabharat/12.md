# Canto 12

1–11. Then, when the sun, friend of the lotus, ending the grief of the chakravaka parted from its mate, bright as a bandhuka flower, was about to climb the peak of the eastern mountain; when the courtyard of the sky was reddened by the glow of dawn; when the quarters had been freed from the night-prowler darkness; when the breeze blew, cooled by spray, gently stirring the Pampa lake and sweet with the scent of blooming lotuses — then, at the order of Mustafa, these men surrounded King Shah's camp: Dilavar, Masud, Sarja Yakut, Ambar; the lord of Adavani and the lord of Karnapura; Farad and the two brothers Khairat; Yakut, Atash, Azam, Bahlol, the proud Malik Rahan; Raghav and Mamba; Vedoji, son of Bhaskar; the very strong Ballal, son of King Haibat; three Brahmin brothers mad with pride, of the army; the Pawars Siddha and Mamba, the strong Bhosle; and other army captains born of various families. Maddened by the lust of battle, bearing great weapons, they seemed to shake the sky with their mass of banners, to pound the earth with the tips of their prancing horses' hooves, to burn up the whole three worlds with their masses of energy, to build a wall all around with their strong forces, blazing like fire and fearless as Death.

12–13. Its elephants unsaddled, its horses unharnessed, its soldiers not ready, its commanders asleep, its sentries drowsy from the night's watch — that camp of King Shah was thrown into confusion by many fears.

14. As the sun's disc is ringed by a great halo, so Shah's camp was ringed by the enemy army.

15. Mustafa himself, lord of the troops, guarded the rear of that army roaring like the ocean on every side.

16–20. Then, with his kinsmen Khandoji, Ambaji and Manaji around him, and surrounded by men bearing swords, bows, lances, javelins and discs, the strong-armed Baji Raje Ghorpade, of terrible deeds; Jasvant Wadve, another submarine fire; Maloji, ornament of the Pawar family; and the famous King Tuloji of the Bhosle line — all these strongest of the strong, pounding the earth with the hooves of the horses they rode, entered Shah's camp.

21. Roaring loudly, eager to conquer and drunk with pride, they woke the sleeping Shah, as elephants might wake a lion.

22. As soon as he woke and heard that the enemy had come, Shah, while arming himself, ordered his soldiers loudly: "Get ready!"

23. Then a cry of "Horse, horse! Sword, sword! Lance, lance!" broke out in the camp of Shah's men.

24–25. Then, while the strong-armed and mighty Shah was arming, and his great army was gripped by sudden fear, Khandoji Patil, like a lion whose course is never checked in battle after battle, rode alone against the Ghorpades.

26–27. Wearing armour, carrying a glorious shield of rhinoceros hide, sure of hand, lance in hand, sword at his side, knowing the work of war — when he rode quickly onto the field, Baji Raje and the others then gave lion-roars.

28. Then the Ghorpade captains, roused to fury, surrounded that Patil, the crest-jewel of his family, with anger.

29. Then the radiant man whirled his lance, which licked the clouds, and made his horse turn thousands of circles.

30. In the space around that whirling lance, a circle of light shone like the disc of the sun.

31. With that lance he furiously cut to pieces the great warriors who struck at him there with their various weapons.

32. As mountains fell when the angry wielder of the thunderbolt struck them, so many men fell to the ground, every limb broken.

33. Though struck hard by other warriors biting their lips in anger, he showed his manhood.

34. Then with his long, sharp lance he cut off someone's head, like a mountain peak.

35. In the same way, in his fury, that best of the strong pierced someone's chest, broad as a slab of rock and impenetrable.

36. With a blow of the blade of his lance he at once struck down a horseman's shoulder, high as an elephant's temple.

37. Khandoji cut off one man's feet, another's waist, another's throat.

38. Leaping up again and again and striking down enemies all around with his lance, the Patil, though alone, seemed there to be many.

39. Wounded by hundreds of sharp weapons, his armour broken by the host of attacking enemies, he drove back the elephant-like foes.

40. His body cut by the arrows his enemies shot, his limbs wet with blood, he shone like the red planet Mars.

41. Seeing the wonderful skill of the Patil as he fought, Baji Raje and the other warriors roared and slapped their arms.

42. In the battle Manaji, breaker of pride, cut his lance in two; and he took a cruel sword in his hand.

43. Then, seeing him sword in hand and sure of hand, the strong-armed Baji Raje smiled and challenged him to a great duel.

44. Then the Patil struck at Baji Raje's chest with a sword like Death's rod.

45–47. When the strong-armed Baji Raje, his broad chest struck by that great blow, fell into a swoon, Ambaji hurled a terrible iron club at Khandoji, Manaji a mace, Mallaji a sharp spear like a tongue of flame, Balaji a matchless lance, Jasvant an arrow, and Khandoji a sword.

48–49. All the weapons of the warriors fell on that terrible man from every side; with his own sword he cut some in two, some in three, some in five, some in nine and some in ten.

50. Baji Raje, after experiencing for a moment a swoon and for a moment a state beyond all description, fought the Patil again.

51. Then with his skill Baji Raje brought down on the Patil a huge mace that struck fear into his enemies.

52–56. When, struck by that cruel mace of Baji Raje as the Krauncha mountain was by the spear of Karttikeya, the fine warrior Khandoji Patil had fallen and ridden off in the chariot of the gods, and his own army was in turmoil, being slaughtered by the enemy army — then the strong-armed great King Shah, with his helmet tied on, his turban fluttering, carrying a shield, in armour, tall, best of bowmen, a lance-fighter who had mastered the art of wheeling in circles, mounting a great horse like a cloud riding on a cloud, suddenly attacked Baji Raje, wanting to kill him.

57–59. The king of the Abhiras, Dasaji, conqueror of the ten quarters; Yogaji Bhandakar, bearing bow and arrows; Santaji Gunjavatkar, Meghaji Thakur, his brother Tryambakraj, and the proud Dattaraj — these and other captains of the army, and many guards of the rear, protected King Shah on every side by the hundred.

60–62. King Baji Raje and the others saw him: filling the sky with the twang of his drawn bow, a hero with the neck of a lion and the shoulders of a bull, of great vigour, guarded by his two brothers Datta and Tryambak, surrounded by his soldiers as Shatakratu by the gods, and suddenly held back by a contrary wind.

63. Seeing the enemies with their raised weapons, King Shah roared like a lion, making earth and sky resound.

64. The quarters were filled with Shah's roar, and the ocean, at once echoing it, was stirred.

65. Baji Raje could not bear Shah's roar, as a maddened elephant cannot bear the trumpeting of another maddened elephant.

66–67. Then the Ghorpades, roaring with a terrible thunderous noise, came up calling out their own names — men of great power, in fine armour, skilled in action — and surrounded Shah as clouds surround the moon.

68–69. Then the two kings Tryambak and Datta, Dasaji, the strong-armed Meghaji, bright as Mahasena, Yogaji, the Gunjavatkar and others, wishing to protect King Shah, attacked the enemy warriors.

70–72. Then Manaji fought Tryambakraj, bow in hand; Khandoji, conqueror of the wicked, fought Dattaraj; Mallaji, conqueror of rivals, fought Yogaji; Ambuji fought Meghaji, who had a voice like thunder; Baji Raje fought the valiant King Shah; and many others fought many other long-armed men.

73. Twanging their bows, eager to conquer one another, maddened with pride, the great warriors danced like actors on the stage of battle.

74. Foot soldiers fought foot soldiers there, horsemen horsemen, and many elephant riders elephant riders, each wanting to conquer.

75–77. Spearmen struck spearmen, those of firm grip those of firm grip, club-men with terrible clubs, mallet-men with mallets, swordsmen with swords, javelin-men with sharp javelins, mace-men with maces, men with discs by the thousand with discs, archers with sharp arrows, dagger-men with daggers, lancers with lances: so they then struck one another in great numbers.

78–80. Heads with their helmets, chests with their armour, hands with their gauntlets, arms with their armlets, banners with their staffs, bows with their arrows, horses with their riders, elephants with their drivers — these, cut down by the weapons hurled by the enemy, fell to the ground then by the many hundreds on both sides.

81. Headless trunks ran about here and there, drawing bows, with guards on their fingers.

82. Alas! Whoever had cut off a man's head or any limb, the trunk ran at him in that very instant and attacked him.

83–90. Then, as pools of blood formed from the bodies of horses, elephants and men cut apart by sharp arrows; as the ground grew slippery with marrow, flesh, fat and lard; as the hosts of dakinis rejoiced with dancing goblins; as the circle of Bhairavis, with ear-rings made from the skulls of this and that captain, grew wild with drink; as the moon-crested god rejoiced with his goblins, made glad by the lovely garlands of the heads of heroes who had pierced the disc of the sun — Datta Raje, alas, had his hand cut off there by King Khandoji; King Tryambak, alas, was beaten by King Manaji; Meghaji, struck by the blows of weapons hurled by Ambuji, was terrified; Dasaji too fled; his own army was failing and confused, like an ocean being drunk by the sage Agastya; and the Bhandakar was harassed by Malaji with showers of arrows. Then King Shah covered King Baji with sharp arrows.

91. Though his body was torn by those very sharp arrows, Baji Raje, of great energy, did not faint.

92. Then, seeing King Shah there with his glittering armour, he struck him on the arm with his lance.

93. Skilled in the science of war, his body hard as a thunderbolt, King Shah was not troubled by that lance blow.

94–95. Then the valiant great King Shah was surrounded by fighters armed with arrows, spears, maces, swords, lances and other weapons — Manaji and the others, their anger rising, guarding Baji Raje and showing their own valour.

96. There the valiant lord of the earth scorched them all with arrows whose touch was like the flame of the fire at the end of time.

97–98. Then hundreds of soldiers, cut to pieces by Shah's arrows, their arms broken off, red with flowing blood, fell before the eyes of Baji Raje, like flowering palasha trees struck down at once by a fierce gale.

99. Wherever he rode, mounted on his leaping horse, there thousands of his enemies fled in terror.

100. Struck down by blows like thunderbolts, they were shattered like mountains into ten, a hundred, a thousand pieces.

101–102. Then one man cut down Shah's attendant, another his companion, one his matchless armour, another his shield with its crescent boss; then one Ghorpade cut his quiver and his bow, and another his tall banner.

103–106. Then, when the great and strong-armed king — showered by the rain of weapons hurled by the enemy, quickly leaping from his horse, moving like the enemy of serpents, his limbs red with the blood flowing from his body pierced by arrows, bewildered by the strain of the great battle — embraced the ground; and when the enemy host was roaring and slapping its arms; then a cry of grief rose loudly in the Bhosles' army.

107–109. There, as the steady, righteous King Shah, glorious as Dharmaraja — the very treasure, as it were, of all the world and the refuge of all — lay sunk on the ground in the great waves of a swoon, like the sun fallen from the sky by fate, the strong Balaji quickly leapt from his horse and, showing himself a kinsman, protected him with his own shield. Then King Baji himself took the strong-armed Shah,

110. as a snake-charmer seizes a hissing serpent; and then the enemies set him on an elephant and led him away.

111–112. Seeing him fainting, his mouth dry, his eyes closed, like a lion cast into a cage, King Shah, deceived by the evil-minded Mustafa, a master of deceit, the people lamented loudly.

113. We think that Shah, though he knew everything, did not see the deceit with which the Muslim covered him as with a cloth.

114. Ah! Mustafa, wanting to do his master's business, to win his trust swore by his own son and broke his oath on his own Book.

115. So the people everywhere then condemned that Muslim; but he, who wanted only his master's business done, thought he had done well.

116–118. Not a saddle, not a horse, not an elephant, not a camel; not a weapon nor a man to bear it; not an instrument nor a player; not a couch, not a canopy, not a flag, not a banner; nothing to sell, no seller, no fuel, no peg; no tent-cloth, not even a pavilion was left there: such, in half a moment, did Shah's camp become.

119. Mahmud rejoiced greatly when he heard from his messengers that Mustafa and the others, like the fire at the end of the world, had captured in battle the proud Shah, as the wielder of the thunderbolt's foes are taken by the guardians of the quarters, and were bringing him under guard.

*Thus ends the twelfth canto in the Samhita, the Anupurana brought to light by Kavindra Paramananda.*
