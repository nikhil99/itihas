# Canto 20

*The prince of poets said:*

1. Meanwhile the blessed Tulaja, who loves her devotees, showed her own form to Shiva, conqueror of the world.

2–8. Then the king saw standing before him the mother of the universe, the giver of boons, like a fresh wish-granting creeper: wearing jewelled ornaments, her feet red, glowing like a champak flower; dressed in a shining blue garment, lovely with dark braided hair; most delicate of limb, in the dress of a young girl; her face like the full moon, her eyes like blue lotuses; softly smiling, her lips like fresh coral; a bright saffron mark on her forehead; jewelled ear ornaments; a jewelled nose-ring set in her lovely nose; soft creeper-like arms, lotus hands, fine fingers; jewelled bracelets crowded with clusters of gems; auspicious marks; clusters and half-clusters of pearls and gems hanging on her throat; a bodice embroidered with golden flowers; a necklace hanging to her navel; and a girdle of jewels.

9. Then, overwhelmed with the joy of seeing her, he bowed again and again with reverence to that primal power.

10. As he bowed, the goddess, the lady of the world, raised him with her own hand and looked on her devotee with eyes full of compassion.

11. Then, after he had praised her with her thousand names and stood before her, the goddess with the lovely teeth and smiling face said to him:

*The goddess said:*

12. "This bull among Muslims named Afzal, who is coming near, is a cheat, my child, and wants to fight you.

13. "Know this arrogant Muslim to be a demon, the root of the tree of the Kali age, hostile to the gods.

14–15. "Him, whom none of the gods can kill, like a second ten-headed one, I have deluded with my maya, together with all his army. Know, lord of the earth, that he who is coming to you together with Death is already slain.

16. "Him, who rises up again and again to stop dharma here, strike to the ground with a great sword stroke.

17–18. "Let all the gods see him fallen on the ground, his limbs wet with streams of flowing blood, headless, his arms scattered, his legs splayed out, a heap of entrails dragged out by vultures, jackals, owls and crows.

19. "When you became Vasudeva, I came down from heaven into Nanda's house to help you.

20. "Now too, enemy of the demons, know that I have left Tulajapur and come of my own accord to help you.

21. "As I was insulted long ago by the fool Kamsa, so have I been insulted now by this sinner.

22. "The creator has ordained that his death shall come by your hand. So, O king, I shall take the form of your sword and stay with you."

23. Saying this, Sharvani entered his sword. Though he was awake, he thought it all a dream.

24. Then, meditating again and again in his heart on the goddess Tulaja, he felt that he had already taken away the whole burden of the earth.

25. Sitting in a hall on the rampart at the top of Pratapgad, he called the commanders of his infantry and said:

26. "Called by me, the Muslim, a man of great backing, is coming here with his army, wishing to make peace.

27. "So today, at my order, let the enemy army come and see this forest without being stopped by you.

28. "He wants to meet me in private. If he hears that you are with me, armed, he might even suspect danger.

29. "So all of you, though armed and close at hand, go into the depths of the thick forest without being seen by the enemy.

30. "If, even after making peace of his own accord, he does not keep it, then let my kettledrums sound and fall upon that army.

31. The king of kings gave them this order privately, as if it were a secret; and the enemy too, with his army, reached that road.

32. His elephants, their knees scraped by the rough stones and their bellies sinking, somehow climbed that mountain with great effort.

33. Driven on by their drivers, the elephants, frightened of falling, clasped with their trunks the clumps of trees beside the path.

34. When the great loads were taken off their backs, the young elephants, though lightened, thought their own bodies a burden.

35. The riders, though their horses were led slowly by the bridle, had a hard time climbing down from them.

36. Alas! Some, afraid of falling into the chasms, held on to shrubs with their hands; the shrubs came away by the roots, and they fell face down.

37. People standing below were crushed by great stones dislodged by the feet of the climbing rut-maddened elephants as they fell.

38. Caught in the tangles of creepers and the thickets of reeds, the army lost its banners and its parasols.

39. Some, terrified at the touch of the trunks of very rut-maddened elephants, killed themselves there by leaping from the cliffs.

40. Some, falling with hands and feet slipping down to the very edge of the high cliffs, dragged many others with them.

41. Having climbed the mountain in this way, though utterly exhausted, the whole Muslim army steadily made up its mind to climb down the far side.

42. The elephants, having climbed the mountain like mountains themselves, climbed down slowly, slowly letting go of their pride.

43. As those who climb a mountain see the world of the gods, so the world, as it climbed down, seemed to see the underworld.

44–47. Then Afzal, as he went, saw Jayavalli: deep like a cave in the middle of the mountains; full of tigers, lions, bears, wolves and wild boar; dark as if made of clouds with its forests of vajra and indra trees; like a poison creeper sprung up in the ocean of darkness; endowed with the qualities of the underworld that crush the earth's pride; always protected by Shiva, conqueror of the three worlds; its floor never touched by the rays of the sun, thick with shade.

48. The strong Afzal, having come near Jayavalli, thought in his heart: "I have taken this."

49. King Shiva too, hearing that he had come near Jayavalli, thought: "By fate he has come into the palm of my hand."

50. Then the enemy, as if checked and closed in by fear, camped on the bank of the Kumudvati, choked with clumps of bamboo.

51. In that fearsome place where his soldiers grew afraid, Afzal alone, being proud, felt no fear.

52–55. That army there — adorned with tall, raised pavilions; its rutting elephants held in check by chains and posts; with lines of Sindhi horses tethered to pegs; crowded at once with many camels brought together; its bazaars lined with shops full of goods; filled with the noise of people coming and going in every direction — though very large, seemed to lie in that forest as if it were small, and seen, it seemed hardly to be seen.

56. Then both Shiva and Afzal sent their own envoys to each other to ask after each other's health.

57. Shiva understood Afzal's heart, and Afzal Shiva's. The creator knew which plan would work; people knew only the terms of the treaty.

58. "To this guest who has come to me I must certainly give presents, and to his men too, out of regard for his wishes.

59–60. "To Muse Khan, Ankush, Yakut and Ambar, and to Hasan Khan and the others, each separately; and likewise to the elder uncle Mambaji. And there must be inspection of the jewels and the craftsmen besides."

61. When the king had summoned merchants in this way, they came at Afzal's order to Shiva.

62. Then those merchants showed Shiva, who wished to buy, the various jewels they had each brought with them.

63. He called those merchants from Afzal's camp, took all the jewels, and kept them all with him. Then the greedy fools, their judgement struck by fate and urged on by their great greed, did not realise that all of them on the mountain top were hemmed in on every side.

64. "King Shiva trusts me; so, going near him now on the pretext of a treaty, I shall drive my hidden dagger deep into his belly and at once spread terror even in the temples of the gods."

65. When Shiva learned that the Muslim had planned this deceit in his own heart, he made ready in battle to give him the full fruit of it. All of this I shall tell as it happened; for its story is sweet as nectar, and beside it talk of nectar is idle.

*Thus ends the twentieth canto, called "The Coming of Afzal", in the Samhita of a hundred thousand verses, the Anupurana of the line of the Sun, brought to light by Kavindra Paramananda.*
