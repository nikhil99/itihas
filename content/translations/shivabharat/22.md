# Canto 22

*The prince of poets said:*

1. By the sound of that kettledrum, spreading like a messenger, King Shiva's soldiers were at once alerted.

2–5. Kamaloji Solaskar, Yesaji Kank, Tanaji Malusare, Kondaji Varkhal and Ramaji Pangarkar — these five, fiery as the five fires, each surrounded by a thousand foot soldiers; Narayana the Brahmin, surrounded by five thousand; and other great fighters famous for their warrior deeds — came at speed and blocked on every side the enemy army, crowded with many fighters, lying in the depths of the forest.

6–7. Meanwhile the enemy too, roused suddenly by the loud roll of the drum, learned that Afzal had been killed there. As those proud men were arming, the foot soldiers who had come down from the slopes of the mountain blocked them on every side as they hurried to arm.

8–9. Like elephants without rut, serpents without venom, men without turbans, mountains without peaks, clouds without water, kings without wealth, the Muslims did not shine then without Afzal.

10. Hearing that their father Afzal had fallen, like a planet falling from the sky, his three sons there lost their wits.

11. Then, like men whose ship is broken by a contrary wind on the ocean, they gave up hope of life in that deep forest.

12–14. Then, seeing all his own soldiers in the midst of the army in distress, their pride and strength gone, running away, Muse Khan the Pathan — devoted to his master, powerful in battle, very wrathful, his nostrils quivering, his eyes broad as lotuses, his arms like an elephant's trunk, fierce, with a terrifying voice — steadied them and himself said:

*Muse Khan said:*

15. "Afzal has gone, counting his own life as a straw in his wish to do his master's business. Have all of us gone too?

16. "The roads are closed off by the mountains on every side. Where will you go? Stand, and strike the enemies surrounding you on every side!

17. "Shame on the life of those who leave their master, friend or comrade, run away, and live!

18. "How can a man who protects himself without doing his master's business show his face among his own people?

19. "Holding this terrible sword, great as Death, I shall now cut down this enemy army by force."

20. Having said this, the strong-armed man mounted a great horse and, surrounded by his own army, quickly came out of the camp.

21. Following him came the strong Hasan, swift as the wind; and the other captains of the army, each with his own troops.

22–23. The ground, broken here and there with many spread-out stones, full of sparks flying from the blows of horses' hooves, crowded with people stumbling, slipping and falling, shook greatly as they moved.

24. Then, seeing the enemy warriors, the horsemen, though stumbling, urged their horses forward even over the stony paths.

25. Then the swift Muse Khan, very angry, spurring his horse forward, met the enemy soldiers with arrows.

26. Hasan and all the other angry bowmen likewise harassed with arrows the enemies before them.

27–31. Then the very dense forest, filled with an uproar so loud that it reached the vault of the sky — with the angry neighing of their horses, the trumpeting of great elephants in the fury of battle, the sharp sound of kettledrums beaten with fierce sticks, the terrible din of instruments of many kinds, and the cries of the beasts and birds of the forest — bears, hyenas, tigers, wolves and rhinoceroses and the wild boars of the forest — that burst out of the caves with their hearts suddenly shaken, made the arrogant enemy warriors wildly afraid with its echoes.

32. Pierced by sharp arrows, the foot soldiers, unshaken, laid the cavalry on the ground in that battle with the edges of their swords.

33. As the running foot soldiers cut the horses to pieces with their sword edges, they seemed in rivalry to have turned the horsemen into foot soldiers.

34. One man, struck by an enemy weapon, red with blood, fell and at once reached the orb of the sun.

35. One man, both thighs broken by the hero Bhima, at once came to the state of Suyodhana.

36. One hero, cut in two by an enemy who struck from above, fell with his body split apart like Jarasandha.

37. Some, their chests pierced by balls shot from guns, though they still held their bows and bowstrings, were lifeless.

38. One man, bearing sword and shield, showing his skill and striking the mighty, alas, carried great honour.

39. The Muslims trembled greatly before the clouds of Shiva's foot soldiers, from which the lightning of raised swords fell all around.

40. Even Muse Khan, who had boasted with his own mouth before his own fighters, was defeated in battle by those heroes.

41–44. Then, when his horse was killed, his weapons had dropped and his mind was terrified, the proud Muse Khan slipped away from the battle; Yakut too, who had once caused fear, at once took to flight; Hasan, though very wise, sank in distress; Ankush, his strength gone, gripped by fear, quickly crept away on soft feet without his sheath; and Afzal's eldest son, leaving his two cowardly brothers and abandoning the army, took another form and slipped away.

45. Then, as the vast army of Adil, without a leader, fled, Shiva's armies blocked it on every side.

46. "Fight me — Bhosle, brother of King Shah!" Saying this there, Mambaji, alas, was released from the bonds of birth.

47–49. Farad Khan's grandson named Randulla, unbearable to all with his very fierce energy; the bold son of Shona, wrathful, of a rakshasa's valour, with arms long as yokes, proud, firm-bodied and young — he, bowing his own head, which was like a mountain peak, came, alas, under the power of Shiva's army and was taken prisoner.

50–51. Then Ambar Khan's son, called Ambar, afraid, his face pale, wanting to live, laying down his armour, sword, shield, spear and bow, came, alas, under the power of Shiva's army and was taken prisoner.

52. The Ghatge named Rajaji, maddened like a rutting elephant, came, alas, under the power of Shiva's army and was taken prisoner.

53. Those two sons of Afzal whom their own elder brother had abandoned came, alas, under the power of Shiva's army and were taken prisoner.

54–55. Other captains of Adil's army too, their arrows broken — commanders of a thousand, two thousand, three thousand, five thousand, six thousand and seven thousand, splendid as the enemies of the thirty — came, alas, under the power of Shiva's army and were made prisoner.

56. With heroes struck down, falling, fallen, fleeing and fled, that terrible battlefield became worth describing.

57–70. That forest of Jayavalli then looked like a terrifying yogini of the destroyer Hara: strewn here and there in great numbers with nooses, spears, half-moon arrows, broadswords and axes; mallets, iron clubs, tridents and javelins; maces, spears, bows, quivers full of arrows; shields, discs, knives and daggers; coats of mail, helmets, golden sashes; leather gloves and finger-guards, gunpowder flasks; drums and other instruments, flags and banners; titles, parasols and fly-whisks; tall tent-poles and screens, pavilions of many colours, very large and of costly silk; tripods, ewers, platters, cups and spittoons; cups for wine of silver and of gold; and occupied by other great heaps of an army's baggage. In places the faces of the slain showed their teeth bared as if in a smile, their eyes staring; in places the heads lay with their turbans fallen off; in places hands, cut off at the root of the arm, still had their rings on and lay with their soft fingers as if decorated; in places thighs lay severed like thick pillars, and knees and shanks with them; in places vultures with their beaks cut open the chests of horses and men, their wings covering the path; in places it was crowded with jackals that came running in packs; in places circles of dakinis played, throwing about lumps of flesh; in places it was full of goblins whose bodies were smeared with the red blood flowing from the open wounds of the slain, cut through to the very entrails; and in places it held yoginis delighted at gaining garlands for Hara. So the forest earned its name, Jayavalli, "the creeper of victory".

71. Having thus conquered the enemy army, all those commanders of the infantry, delighted and very proud, quickly brought the enemy captains, firmly bound, pushing them forward at every step, and came to see Shiva.

72. Some enemy warriors were struck down in battle, some were driven off in play, and some were made prisoner by the wrathful Shiva. That is no wonder in him who sustains the existence of the three worlds, who takes on the sport of a kshatriya king, who destroys the wicked, removes the fear of the earth and bears up the universe.

*Thus ends the twenty-second canto, called "The Rout of Afzal's Army", in the Samhita of a hundred thousand verses, the Anupurana of the line of the Sun, brought to light by Kavindra Paramananda.*
