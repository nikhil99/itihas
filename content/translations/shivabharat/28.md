# Canto 28

*The learned men said:*

1. When that King Shiva, ornament of the Bhosle line, had gone to Rajgiri, what became of the hill of Pranala? Tell us, man of great wisdom.

*The prince of poets said:*

2. "Shaista Khan himself is in this country, and Johar is there. How can we fight on both fronts?

3. "So today let the hill of Pranala be handed over to Adil, and you set out; another task has come up here.

4. "We will in a moment take from Ali Shah full recompense for that hill. Our word will not prove false."

5–6. When Shiva had instructed him in this way through the mouth of his own envoy, Tryambak Bhaskar, thinking it over in his own heart, though himself bent on battle, honoured his master's command and, as if humbly, handed that hill over to Ali Shah.

7. Then, meeting Johar, he asked after his welfare and was greatly honoured by him, who wished for Shiva's friendship.

8. Then, surrounded on every side by his many supporting troops, he came to his master, bowed his head and gazed on him.

*The learned men said:*

9. "Do not kill him; this counsel of mine must be followed. There is another cause for the destruction of this Johar" —

10. so the goddess had come and said to the wise King Shiva earlier, while he was on the hill of Pranala.

11. Then tell us all of it, prince of poets, for you are skilled: by what cause and in what way did he meet his death?

12. Drinking again and again these words of yours on the bank of the Bhagirathi, we are never sated, as the gods are never sated with nectar.

*The prince of poets said:*

13–15. When the strong-armed King Shiva, ordered by the goddess to defeat the bannered army of the copper-faced men himself, had at once broken the strong Abyssinian's array and gone out, and the hill of Pranala had come back by fate into the hand, the utterly foolish Ali Shah, thinking otherwise in his heart, grew angry, they say, with Johar for a long time.

16. "Evil-minded one, greedy as you are, you took much wealth from him; and though you knew he was leaving, you ignored it.

17. "While you were besieging him, I think it would have been hard for that king to get out without your consent.

18. "So give the wealth — give what that king gave you. Otherwise your death will come by my own hand."

19. Adil sent him this message through a letter-bearer; yet that best of strong Abyssinians was not afraid of him.

20. When Adil could not carry that war through, Johar at once took refuge in Karnapura, the most inaccessible of forts.

21. Then, by some device or other, without anyone knowing, Adil had Johar given halahala poison with his food.

22. Ah, how great was the folly of Adil Shah, who did ill to Johar, who had done him good!

23–24. If, by the grace of the goddess, the very hard-to-withstand King Bhosle, whose course was more than human, bewildered that enemy army ringed tight around him and left the mountain, then, we think, Johar was not at fault.

25–26. But this is a digression; now hear what was first begun. King Shiva's history, lovely as the moon and swelling like the ocean of nectar, will be told in detail. Having heard it, let all the learned keep it in their hearts.

27–28. As soon as the strong King Bhosle, having overcome by his own might the Abyssinians who ringed him in, reached Shivapattana, the strong copper-faced armies showed their valour and made a wonderful assault on the fort called Sangramdurg (Chakan).

29. Hearing that news at Shivapattana, King Shiva, foremost of those who know all policy, said this to his ministers:

*King Shiva said:*

30. "Because I was busy with another task and was far away, the city of Chakravati has gone, together with Sangramdurg.

31. "Even now I am ready to go myself and take back from them that city with its fort, though it is hard to take.

32. "But if another task has come up first, we shall all strive for that instead.

33. "Here, no man without helpers can ever defeat the armies of his enemies.

34. "So a wise king must always keep, with great effort, an army that restrains the enemy.

35. "Even a great king without much wealth cannot gather armies of that kind.

36. "With wealth one's purpose grows great; with wealth dharma grows; and from wealth, the third aim, desire, arises. So wealth is praised.

37. "Family, character, youth, learning, manliness, truthfulness, knowledge of virtue and depth — all come from wealth alone.

38. "Through wealth this world and the next are surely won; a man without wealth, though alive, does not truly live.

39. "He who has wealth has friends; he who has wealth has manliness; for him who has wealth all become helpers.

40. "So, like Prithu, I shall by my power milk this earth and carry off the wealth on which this world depends.

41. "Right after that I shall take my revenge there on the uncle of the lord of Delhi, as Agastya did on the ocean."

42. When the lord of the earth, sitting in the assembly, spoke these fitting words, his ministers, schooled in policy, said to him:

*The ministers said:*

43. "When you say that wealth gives power, you speak truly. Who, standing on the vow of a mere wrangler, would deny it?

44. "Wealth gives power to the powerful, but to the powerless it brings no power; in the hands of the powerless, wealth is not gain but only harm.

45. "Whatever wealth there is in all the worlds is yours alone, we know. So, mighty King Shiva, go out on your campaign.

46–48. "Moreover, the general, the uncle of the lord of Delhi, having now conquered Sangramdurg and grown very great-minded, has advanced and is staying at Punyapura. Hearing that you, strong-armed one, are at Shivapattana, he is very much afraid in his heart, and will probably send an army down the Sahya hills by that very road.

49. "Arrangements must first be made so that, as it marches on, it cannot get down from the Sahya; then, by all means, the other matter must be taken up."

50. Hearing these timely words of the ministers, that great-minded man, whose deeds were blessed, thought them right.

*The learned men said:*

51. And what, alas, did Shaista Khan do, staying at Punyapura with seven times seven thousand horsemen?

*The prince of poets said:*

52. He called the capable Muslim named Kartalab, who stood before him, and said this to him in private:

*Shaista Khan said:*

53. "Your father Jasvant, of the Uzbek line, is valiant, and you too have spent your life only in war.

54. "You conquered the strong Galib, and now by the strength of your own arm took Prachandapur and gave it to me here.

55. "Since even the lord of the Sahya hills, so hard to withstand in battle, finds it hard to do the work that you do — that is known.

56. "Unless we march over the Sahya, the lord of the Sahya, unconquered and proud, will never come under our control.

57. "So today, at my command, with your army, alas, set your mind on descending the Sahya hills by force.

58. "From today, hero, I shall be in your debt. Climb down the Sahya hills and give me great glory.

59. "Champavati (Chaul), Kalyanpur and Bhimapuri, Panvel and Nagothane: you must bring them under control.

60–62. "Kachchap and Chauhan, mighty men with great weapons; Amarsingh and Mitrasen with his brothers; Sarjerao of the Gadhe line, and the Raybagin, hard to withstand; Jasvant Kokate and the strong-armed Yadav — these honoured men, whom I have sent with their armies, will follow you as the demons followed Virochana.

63. "Go before all the armies, intent and undistracted; I shall guard your rear, you who do what I hold dear."

64. Ordered in this way by that general, this hero of famous valour set out, surrounded by heroes.

65. Then, alas, taking the road south and north from Lohagad, he fearlessly began the descent of the Sahya hills.

66. Going by that single-file path, narrow as a gun barrel, the army was halted, as it were, at every step.

67. "We shall fall, face down, from here" — so certain were they, as the men of the copper-faced army climbed down from the Sahya.

68. Kartalab, given over to pride and joined with his own army, saw neither the killer nor the destroyer, but only the forest.

69. When he entered that forest, full of enemies yet seeming empty, Mitrasen and the others did not leave his side.

70. In that great forest where not even the wind blew, the Muslim, staying there, gave no thought to how to protect himself.

*The learned men said:*

71. Those enemies of King Shiva, entering that forest, thought "We are seeing a world without light."

72. Why did Shiva not at once stop that enemy as he was climbing down from the Sahya, puffed up by a contrary wind?

73. That country of King Shiva lies at the foot of the Sahya. How was it overrun then by the enemies eager to take it?

*The prince of poets said:*

74. If Shiva had stopped the enemy at the very start, he would not have fallen with his armies into that ocean of a forest.

75. Having decided this, the king, though able, did not stop him there, swollen with pride in the strength of his arm.

76. Then, as he came on further and further below the Sahya, Shiva advanced and shut in that enemy in the middle of the road.

77–78. The commanders of foot soldiers, armed, whom that heroic king had stationed beforehand there on both sides at every point within the forest, the soldiers of the lord of Delhi did not perceive, even though they were stationed near them.

79. Then Kartalab, with his armies, entered the road that runs through the dense forest of Umbarkhind.

80. Then a great sound of war-drums suddenly booming arose, and told the enemy fighters: "Shiva!"

81. Hearing the boom of Shiva's drums, Kartalab, alas, summoned his own heart to the heroic mood.

82–84. Then the strong-armed Mitrasen, lord of Tungaranya, quickly dismounted from his horse and took his stand on the ground, surrounded by his own heroes, in the hero's posture; he readied his bow, set an arrow to it, and stood eager to destroy the enemy. Amarsingh and the others too stood ready for battle like him.

85. Then, quickly realising that the enemy was striking there at every step with cannon balls, the mighty Muslim, alas, gathered his own army and stood.

86. Then, in the middle of the forest, the steady bowman Amarsingh, bright with the fine young warriors around him, struck down the army of the hostile king with flights of arrows, and gave the battle great colour.

87. "Alas! Do not be afraid in any way; stand firm," he cried; and Mitrasen at once checked the enemy army with the hosts of arrows set to his matchless bow, as the Muslim army fled from forest to forest.

88. Some of the enemy's warriors, struck suddenly by the arrows shot by Amarsingh and Mitrasen, their limbs bathed in streaming blood, fell into a swoon.

89. "This great army of the enemy, which has come into this very fearsome forest in its anger, will not give up its passion for battle. So you must at once block every road it could take" — so King Shiva himself told his general.

90. Then, quickly mounting his horse to kill his enemies, drawing in his hand the bow that raised his greatness in the world, he seemed to gods and demons not a whit less than Pritha's son, who burned the Khandava forest with the flames of blazing fire.

91. The forest, its heart filled with the flood of blood from the enemy horses felled by the sharp, long swords of Shiva's fine warriors, was made to look red as the dawn.

92. Though Mitrasen and the others guarded it at every step without wavering, the army of Indraprastha, caught in a cage of the enemy's arrows, sank into a plight that is well known.

*Thus ends the twenty-eighth canto in the Samhita of a hundred thousand verses, the Anupurana of the line of the Sun, brought to light by Kavindra Paramananda of Nidhivasa.*
