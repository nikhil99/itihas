# Canto 31

*The prince of poets said:*

1. Then Malusare went out with his armies to meet the lord Shiva, who had quickly come back from Rajapur, and saw him.

2–3. As that proud man bowed, the king, seeing Tanaji, who had defeated the enemy army with his great might, and who was presented to him in his presence by the son of King Nilakantha, honoured him and his army with very great favour.

4. Then, hearing of that insolence of Suryaraja, the swift Shiva, though very angry, at once restrained his anger.

5. Then a herald, quickly sent by King Shiva, went to the lord of Prabhavali and spoke these words of honour:

*The herald said:*

6. "Whenever you joined to help Adil, you committed many offences against King Shiva.

7. "And now you have fearlessly fallen with your army at night upon Shiva's army, which was at Sangameshvar.

8. "Lord of Prabhavali, tell me, how is that great offence of yours to be endured by the son of Shah, conqueror of the world?

9. "So, though very angry with you, by good fortune he has been merciful to you; hear what he has commanded you today, as I tell it.

*King Shiva said:*

10–11. "'Strong-armed one, the strong-armed lord of men who came to you in terror and ran away like a mad wild elephant — the lord of Palivan, who had offended me — I have now quickly taken over his territory.

12. "'So you need not be afraid of me. Come there to Palivan, where I shall grant you safety.

13. "'If out of pride you do not come there, you will come to the same state as he. No one will protect you from my anger today.

14. Then, hearing from the herald Shiva's words spoken in this way, the king of Shringarpur said to him, "Go; I am coming."

15. Then, coming near Palivan, he reported in private to King Shiva the words Suryaraja had spoken.

16. Then Shiva, overrunning that country named Palivan, favoured those who deserved favour and punished those who deserved punishment.

17–23. Brahmins, kshatriyas, vaishyas and shudras; brass-workers, goldsmiths, ironsmiths and coppersmiths; carpenters, masons, barbers and door-keepers; garland-makers, potters, artisans and actors; weavers, tailors, dyers and many oil-pressers; betel-sellers, liquor-sellers, washermen and distillers; goatherds, cowherds, temple servants and farmers; perfumers, confectioners and shell-workers; drummers, flute-players, hand-drummers and lute-players; experts in music, hunters and sword-polishers; money-lenders, arrow-makers, snake-charmers and tundika players; shoemakers, Pulindas, fishermen and wandering folk — all these, who had fled in fear, at once came back to that country, and their fortunes grew day by day as they rejoiced in many ways.

24–25. Then, seeing a mountain, very able to protect that country, famous as Chiradurg, the zealous man had craftsmen surround its top all around with a tall, strong rampart.

26. And, to show that it was the ornament of that region, the lord of the earth gave this mountain the name Mandana (Mandangad).

27–28. Then, placing in that very inaccessible mountain fort a fort-commander unassailable and skilled in the work of guarding, and some army endowed with every virtue, the lord, intent, set out, wishing to conquer the king of Shringarpur.

*The learned men said:*

29–30. Why did the lord Shiva not go at once to Shringarpattana to overpower the lord of Prabhavali? And why did he send an envoy to him, who was proud and hostile, saying, "I give you safety"?

*The prince of poets said:*

31–32. Long ago, thinking Shiva, famed for the deed of the victory at Jayavalli, to be of a valour equal to Indra's, Suryaraja, wishing to keep the large realm of Prabhavali, sent word by his envoy: "I am your bought son."

33. From that time Shiva, the refuge of those who take refuge, long protected that king, who was turned towards refuge.

34. Whenever King Shiva fought his enemies, Suryaraja then gave him help.

35–36. Then Suryaraja, his mind ruined by fate, his fear cast off, living in the dense forest, crooked in his ways, gave help again and again, secretly and openly, in many ways, to the enemies of King Shiva, who did not know him for what he was.

37. Though he offended again and again, and though he deserved punishment, Shiva, firm in his vows, did not think of punishing him.

38. But when, though called, he did not come into his presence out of pride, then King Shiva, the Bhosle, grew angry with Suryaraja.

39. Then that lord of the earth set out on the march with ten thousand swift foot soldiers.

40. Then the mighty one, mounted in a litter and going in haste, saw before him the town of Saravara.

41. Then the lord of Prabhavali, hearing that Shiva, touched by anger, had come near, said to his own people in dismay:

*Suryaraja said:*

42–43. "The son of King Shah — a man of action, strong-armed, praised by gods, demons and men, joined with many soldiers, a fighter by stratagem — is said to have come close, quite unknown, set on taking Shringarpur from us.

44. "How shall we cross this war with that powerful man, like a shoreless ocean?"

45. Having said this, and approved there by his own people, he set his mind on flight, wishing to save himself.

*The learned men said:*

46–51. He who lived in an impregnable forest fort; whose mind was set almost every day on fighting; whose rule the Sahya mountains, unassailable, obeyed; who had won a throne of former kings hard for others to win; with whom Adil, though offended by that proud man wishing to guard his own realm, made a treaty and so escaped his own plight; who brought the Abyssinians under his control as if they were his own; who made even the ocean humble; through whom his inherited army rejoiced; who was never defeated by anyone through another's might — how was it that this lord of Prabhavali too, the best of those who know the warrior's dharma, did not fight Shiva, who wished to do him harm?

*The prince of poets said:*

52–54. Earlier, hearing that Shiva, of fiery valour like the fire of the world's end, had set out from Sangameshvar towards Palivan, Suryaraja, a man of little sense, thinking himself without a rival and seeing no prospect of battle, gave leave to go home to his commanders of the troops he had gathered, men of lion-like valour, honoured in the world.

55. Then, when Shiva quickly turned back from Palivan, Suryaraja could not gather his army.

56. For this reason, surely, best of the twice-born, the lord of Prabhavali, dejected, did not set his mind on battle.

57–59. By Shiva — incarnation of Vishvaksena, at the head of many soldiers; remover of the pride of the arrogant Adil's arm; crusher of the lord of Delhi's army; of thunderbolt-hard body; unconquerable; of very secret counsel; his own master; of great power; taker of the forts of enemies hard to take; carried along in a litter; breaker of the arms of Chandraraj; slayer of Afzal —

60. — having made great enmity with such a man, Suryaraja set his mind on flight. In this I find nothing to wonder at.

61–68. Then, entering the forest of Shringarpur — its interior crowded with hundreds of tall, stretched-out rocks; resounding with the hoots of owls sitting in the hollows of trees; bright with the many cast skins of great serpents; its sky covered by the canopy of countless trees; lovely with many peacocks dancing nearby; full of the cries of parrots flying out of their nests and the chatter of monkeys; dark as a bank of clouds clinging to the mountain slopes; its leaves stirred moment by moment by the long-tailed monkeys swinging on the branches; resounding with the grunting of boars with bristling hair; full of herds of elephants maddened like Mount Mandara; tigers asleep in the thickets of bamboo; many fearless bears breaking open ant-hills; and, though rough and uneven, made comfortable by its fine huts — that ornament of good warriors, eager to attack in many ways, hearing that Suryaraja had fled, was, with his army, as if disappointed.

69. Then Shiva — who gives safety to those who take refuge, who removes the pride of the proud, whose arm is filled with heroic zeal — came to Shringarpur and looked at it.

70. Without even getting down from the litter he rode in, he quickly entered Shringarpur, which his soldiers had filled in a moment; and as he looked at it, he thought himself unconquerable by his enemies.

71. When that king forcefully trod with his own foot that throne of the hostile king which had been famous as "unconquerable by others", the people thought him very proud indeed.

72. Having received from him a firm promise of safety, the townspeople, who had gathered, entered Shringarpur again, and, prospering, made it a place of great splendour.

73. Then that Shiva, whose valour conquers the people of the three worlds, alas, felt shame at killing the hostile king, who had quickly gone off to the ends of the earth with a few soldiers following him.

*Thus ends the thirty-first canto, called "The Entry into His Own City", in the Samhita of a hundred thousand verses, the Anupurana of the line of the Sun, brought to light by Kavindra Paramananda.*
