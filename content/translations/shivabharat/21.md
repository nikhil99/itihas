# Canto 21

*The prince of poets said:*

1–2. Then, as those two, very eager to see each other, each holding firm in his own policy and with minds keenly attentive, came to an agreement through their envoys, hear it all, best of the twice-born, as I tell it.

3–8. "Leaving his own army where it is, Afzal alone, weapon in hand, shall set out; mounted in a litter he shall come forward, and two or three attendants only may follow to wait on him. Likewise he himself shall come to the foot of Pratapgad, and Shiva shall stay in the hall waiting for him there. Shiva, weapon in hand, shall come up and show his guest due honour and hospitality. Brave men, steady and armed, ten by ten, shall stand behind them at the distance of an arrow's flight, for the protection of both. And when the two have met, they shall hold there a secret talk that will be a great festival for the whole world."

9. Having made this agreement in this way, and each planning a different deceit, the two, eager to see each other, then shone.

10. Then, hearing that he had come to the ground at the foot of Pratapgad, the great King Shiva shone as he made himself ready.

11–18. He worshipped the god of gods, whose banner is the bull, in the many ways his priest had prescribed and as he did every day; performed his daily rite of giving; ate a light meal, wise as he was; sipped water again and again by the rules, pure himself; meditated a moment on the goddess Tulaja in his heart; put on dress suited to the occasion; looked in a mirror at his own face, without equal in the world; at once rose from his seat and bowed to his priest and to other Brahmins, whose blessings filled him with happy joy; touched curds, durva grass and unbroken rice; looked at the orb of the sun; approached a calf standing before him with its mother and gave at once to a virtuous Brahmin a cow adorned with gold; and stationed his valiant followers, ready to follow him, for the protection of Pratapgad. Then, carrying a hidden deceit in his own heart, he went to that Muslim, who had come close, as if to a guest who had come out of affection.

19–20. With his turban and its bright white crest, and his jacket made red with saffron, Shiva, the Bhosle, in his armour, shone; but what need had that man of adamant body of armour?

21. Holding a sword in one hand and a broadsword in the other, he looked like Vishnu himself with the Nandaka sword and the Kaumodaki mace in his hands.

22–23. Then the enemy saw Shiva, steady-eyed and a hero: come down from the edge of the mountain like a lion bounding out, setting his feet down quickly, standing close before him, fearsome with his fine, long, jutting beard pointed like the tip of a goad.

24. Shiva too, seeing him before him as Indra saw Vritra coming, smiled and fixed his own eyes on him.

25–26. To make that clever hero, the son of Shah, standing before him like angry Death, put his trust in him, the wicked man, his heart full of deceit, gave the sword he held in his own hand, its course unblunted, into the hand of the attendant at his side.

27. Then, making a show of friendship in a loud voice, he, struck down by hostile fate, said to him:

*Afzal said:*

28. "Ah, you who wear a false zeal for war and behave most wilfully, why do you abandon the path of policy and take to bad ways?

29. "You do not serve Adil, nor the Qutb, nor the very mighty lord of Delhi. You count no one and swell your pride in your heart.

30. "So today I have come to discipline you, ill-disciplined as you are. Give up these hills, give up your greed, and come to me.

31–32. "I shall hold you by my own hand and take you to Vijayapattana, make you bow your head before Ali Shah, have you bow to that valiant lord, and give you, O king, still greater riches.

33. "Son of King Shah, Shiva, give up your arrogance; take my hand in yours. Come here, embrace me."

34. Saying this, he seized Shiva's neck with his left hand, and with the other drove his dagger into his side.

35–36. Shiva, who knew the arts of wrestling, freed his neck from the grip of that hand, with a deep roar that echoed from the caves; and with his mind unshaken, drawing in his limbs a little, he himself made the dagger miss as it entered his belly.

37–39. "Here is the stroke I give you — take it! Seize me now!" Saying this, steady, with a lion's voice, a lion's gait, a lion's body, a lion's eyes and a lion's shoulders, splendid with the unsheathed weapon raised in his own hand, Shiva, the man of great vows, set on paying back that enemy, struck him in the belly with the point of his sword.

40. Driven quickly into the enemy's belly as far as the back, that sword came out dragging all his entrails with it.

41. As the spear of the angry Karttikeya split the Krauncha mountain, so the creeper of Shiva's sword split Afzal and shone.

42. When King Shiva showed such manhood, Afzal stood there with his head reeling.

43–46. Then, wetting the ground with streams of blood, staggering as if drunk and reeling with a swoon, the Khan, gathering up in his own hand all his entrails as they spilled out from his belly through the wound of Shiva's weapon, cried to the man at his side: "This man has killed me here. Kill him at once!" At that very moment the proud man who held Afzal's sword rushed at King Shiva, wanting only to kill him.

47. Knowing that the king would not kill a Brahmin, the master Afzal had stationed that warrior in the fight.

48. Hearing that he was a Brahmin, Shiva, lord of the earth, who knew right conduct, though he came at him, did not wish to kill him.

49. Before Afzal's soldiers had reached the spot, that man brought his sword down on Shiva.

50. Shiva warded off with his sword the sword brought down on him, and with his broadsword split in two the head of the man's master as well.

51. The enemy's head, cut through by the broadsword, at once fell to the ground like a mountain peak struck by the thunderbolt.

52. There Shiva became Jishnu, Indra himself; the Muslim became a mountain; and the broadsword with its sharp edge became the hundred-edged thunderbolt.

53–55. In one place lay the outstretched trunk, in another the spilled entrails; in one place the fallen head, in another the turban; in one place the scattered parasol, in another the earring; and in another the jewelled crest of pearls and gems. Brought to this state, the wicked man rolled on the face of the earth by his own deed.

56. Before his soldiers could even blink, the lord of men struck down that Muslim with a loud cry.

57–61. Seeing their master slain, the captains of the Muslim army, furious and greatly provoked, brandishing their weapons — Sayyad named Abdul, Bada Sayyad too, Rahim Khan, the arrogant son of Afzal's brother, Pahilvan Khan, very proud and of great family, and the two heroes Pilaji and Shankaraji, born of the Mohite line, and four other Muslims swifter than the wind, strong, crushing, the rear-guards of Afzal — when he was destroyed, they all quickly came together and rushed at King Shiva, wanting to kill him, as the demons rushed at the lord of the gods when Jambha was destroyed.

62. Then, whirling those two weapons of his, he made around himself what seemed a wide rampart.

63. Though he did not whirl round in that battle, whirling only his weapons, he made the eyes of his rival enemies whirl day and night.

64. With his black sword and sharp broadsword he made circles like bracelets all around the quarters.

65–66. As he drew those circles, proud in his own heart, brandishing the broadsword and the sword again and again, neither the enemy nor even his own soldiers could see Shivavarma, of terrible deeds, in that battle.

67. For a moment he entered the earth, for a moment the sky; for a moment he stayed in the middle; not for a moment was he still.

68–69. The many sword blows dealt by enemy heroes, which came at him swift as falling lightning, the lord parried now with the broadsword, now with his firm-gripped sword, now with both at once.

70–73. Then Sambhaji Kavaji, Katuji Ingale, the two of the Kank family, Kondaji and Yesaji, Krishnaji Gaikwad, Surji Kante, Jiva Mahala, Vishvaji Murumbak, Sambhaji Karvar and Ibrahim the Abyssinian — these ten great heroes, guards of King Shiva, roaring and drawing their great swords from their sheaths with fury, opposed them as winds oppose mountains.

74. As their roar and the enemy's filled the hollow of the sky, even the horse of the mountain-splitter Indra, tied in its stable, fled.

75–78. "King, I am striking this blow at you; bear it!" Crying this again and again, Bada Sayyad, roaring like a lion, rushed once more at Afzal's slayer. The swift Jiva Mahala, though forbidden — "I alone will kill this one who has come near me" — did not heed the king's words; he took on his own body the sword Sayyad had raised, and that hero cut Sayyad in two with his sword.

79–80. Kavaji, the two Kanks, the proud Ingale, the hero Murumbak, Karvar, the fierce Kante, Gaikwad and the Abyssinian quickly struck down the others too, as they fought with fury.

81–82. Their shoulders and hands cut off, their feet cut off, their heads and middles cut through, fallen on every side, their bodies, ah, smeared with blood and shining like trees in bloom, lay there in splendour.

83–84. In the Shalivahana Shaka year reckoned as one, eighty, five and ten hundreds — that is, 1581 — in the year named Vikari, in the month of Margashirsha, in the bright half, on the seventh day, a Thursday, at midday, Afzal, enemy of the gods, was killed by Shiva.

*(This corresponds to 10 November 1659.)*

85. When Afzal, strong as he was, had been killed by this king with his strength, a very cool, gentle breeze, fragrant with abundant flowers, blew; the rivers too at once had water clear as nectar, steady and calm; and all the gods, each going to his own place, were at peace.

86. When, having thus struck down his own enemy loudly in battle, King Shiva shone, weapon in hand, there at once arose on the mountain summit the deep sound of the kettledrums, proclaiming victory and filling the three worlds.

*Thus ends the twenty-first canto, called "The Killing of Afzal", in the Samhita of a hundred thousand verses, the Anupurana brought to light by Kavindra Paramananda.*
