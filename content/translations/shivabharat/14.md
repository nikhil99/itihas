# Canto 14

*The prince of poets said:*

1. Hearing that Haibat's strong son had been struck down in battle, the proud Fateh Khan was furious with King Shiva.

2–3. Then, surrounded by strong Muslims led by Muse Khan, guarded by kings led by Mattaji, and with vassals like rut-maddened elephants all around him, he quickly set out, wanting to conquer King Shiva.

4–8. Then that guardian of Adil's earth, eager for battle, quickly gathered and saw before him, at Purandar, an ocean of an army: crowded with the crocodiles of moving, rut-maddened elephants; its waves the lines of tall, restless, leaping horses; glittering with the billows of many banners shaken by the wind; full of clouds of dust stirred from the face of the earth; its shores echoing the deep boom of rolling kettledrums; pale with a foam of white parasols; its tortoises hard shields; its serpents fierce bows; bringing terror like the submarine fire with its flashing weapons.

9. The proud Fateh Khan, moving forward, camped his mighty army not far from that hill.

10. Seeing the enemy army come near, the son of Shah had the war-drum beaten on the summit of the hill.

11. With the sound of that drum he at once shook the minds of the enemy soldiers, as the wind shakes the Manasa lake.

12–14. Then the proud Fateh Khan, surrounded by Muse Khan and other heroes of matching quality and great arrogance, bearing fine swords like the fire of the world's end, with great zeal for war, armoured, mighty, steady, roaring their war cries, at once began to climb the hill of Purandar.

15–16. As that great army climbed, splitting the clouds with its roars, Fateh Khan was at the rear, Muse Khan at the front, the lord of Phaltan on one flank and the Ghatge on the other.

17. Those who had never set foot on the ground, and always went in vehicles, climbed that high hill then, alas, on their own feet.

18. Seeing the enemy climbing the hill on every side, the commanders of Shiva's army roared like angry lions.

19–20. Shiva's brave men by the thousand showered the enemy heavily with masses of burning iron that shot out with a roar, with musket-balls, with many great boulders, with hundreds of fire-arrows and with many sling-stones.

21–24. Great boulders broken from the cliffs, which Shiva's troops, roaring, hurled down — falling from far above, smashing as they struck, raising dust like rain clouds, shooting out showers of sparks as they broke apart — burst into pieces, flew up all around and fell hard on the enemy army.

25–26. Then, by many iron balls that shot from the cannon, flaming, unbearable and terrifying from afar like lightning, Adil's soldiers were cut to pieces, and like birds they flew up into the sky and fed the lines of falcons there.

27–28. At that moment, fire-arrows with their marks, roaring terribly as they fell from the sky, like serpents with mouths blazing with poison-flame, whirled and whirled and burned the circling army of the Muslims.

29. There a single ball shot from a cannon pierced many Muslims, and they fell to the ground.

30. Some, their chests crushed by the stones thrown by the men on the hill, fainted and stayed where they were, halfway up the path.

31–32. Some, stuck to the hillside and broken by huge stones, vomited a great deal of blood, red as cochineal. Carried off by their friends who followed them, they turned back to their own camp, wanting to save their lives.

33–34. Some, their bodies torn by the balls shot from the guns, covered in spurts of blood like the jets of a fountain, burning with fever and confused with loss of breath, as if thirsty, went to be guests of Death.

35. Seeing that army beaten by the enemy, Muse Khan spoke to the warriors of his own race:

*Muse Khan said:*

36. "These falls of huge rocks — are they not portents at every step? Falling from every side, they are striking down the captains of our army.

37. "Ah! How great is the rise of this lord of Purandar, that he himself overpowers even valiant men like us.

38. "For us, whose fame in the world was born of the sword, it would be the greatest shame to run away from here.

39. "Do not set your foot backward. Look ahead at the mountain. Victory hardly ever abandons the man who stands firm in battle.

40–42. Saying this, the great-minded man, with men who never turn back, climbed the hill of Purandar. Then Sharfa Shah with many soldiers of his own, the two Shaikhs, King Mattaji, the strong lord of Phaltan, Fateh Khan's troops and many vassals came on swiftly.

43–44. Seeing that great wave climbing the slope of Purandar like masses of thundering clouds, at once, at their master's command, Goda and the other very swift warriors raised their weapons and met them in every direction.

45. Muse Khan and the others too fearlessly attacked those enemy fighters, as planets attack great planets.

46–47. Then Sadoji, maddened by the strength of his arm, fought Sharfa Shah; Jagtap, born of a family that sets the world right, fought the angry Muse Khan; Bhairav fought Mina and Ratan; and Wagh, who wielded swift weapons, fought the heroic Ghatge.

48. The very proud Kavaji, mace in hand, with a strong force, fought many other soldiers.

49–51. Then, filled with the pride of battle, with arms long as yokes, their bodies covered with fine helmets and armour, warriors in both armies, hostile to each other, brandishing their bows and setting sharp arrows to them, crying again and again "Wait, wait!", fought a wonderful fight on the slope of Purandar.

52. As they struck one another there for Purandar, alas, many could not get down from it, and many could not get up.

53. Watching one another and taking aim, some covered the earth with arrows and others the sky.

54. The arrow that pierced one man's chest and cut off another's arm took off a third man's head, helmet and all.

55. There Goda, wild with rage, rushed in and drove his sharp lance into Muse Khan's chest.

56–57. But the Muslim, with both hands, pulled out from his own chest that sharp, terrible lance which the enemy had driven in, piercing his armour, and in rage, biting his lips with his teeth, at once broke it in two.

58. The chest, wet with blood, adorned him there as a slab of rock reddened with thick red ochre adorns a mountain.

59. Meanwhile the steady fighter Sadoji, carrying shield and sword, came at Sharfa Shah to fight him.

60–62. Both mad for battle, both red-eyed, both armed, both full of zeal, both whirling their swords and lighting up the sky like lightning, both watching for an opening in the other, roaring like lions, showing the moves called bhranta, udbhranta and the rest, the two of them shone.

63. Then, their bodies wounded by each other's sword strokes, in a single moment both were drenched with blood.

64. Then Wagh, a tiger among men, struck the Ghatge, who carried a terrible spear, on his pillar-like arm with a very sharp spear.

65. Then that Ghatge, skilled in war, his body smeared with flowing blood, struck the tiger hard on the arm with a spear.

66. Struck by him, Wagh, like a second tiger, showed his own valour and delighted his own men.

67. Mina and Ratan, drawing their bows to the ear, came at the fearsome Bhairav Chor and covered him with arrows.

68. His body, riddled by the arrows those two shot, looked like a sieve.

69. Though he was one and they were two, the battle there went on for a long time; yet Bhairav Chor won victory at every step.

70. Kavaji, enraged and blazing like fire, laid low hundreds of enemies with great blows of his mace.

71–72. Their fine armour shattered, their chests torn open, suddenly fainting, their weapons slipping from their hands, reddening the ground with blood flowing from every limb, the men who fell on that battlefield looked splendid.

73. Then those fine warriors, their limbs red with flowing blood, shone like mountains wet with red ochre carried by their streams.

74. Shiva too, the hero radiant with heroic splendour, watched that marvel of a battle between his own men and the enemy.

75. Then from the slope of Purandar a great river of red water began to flow, roaring, wild with pride.

76. Mounted in the sky in their aerial cars and lighting up the heavens, all the gods watched that great battle again and again and praised it.

77. Hara came there gladly with his goblins, lovely with a garland of heads that he had made at that moment.

78. The flesh-eating rakshasas, greatly hungry at that moment, found plenty of flesh and were satisfied.

79. Then a ghost, setting a skull fearlessly on its lap, quickly drank its sweet drink mixed with blood.

80. Then the shakinis, together with the dakinis skilled in leaping and dancing about, fed their own bodies on the flesh of the soldiers.

*The prince of poets said:*

81. Now, Brahmins, I shall tell you everything — how Goda killed Muse Khan there. Listen.

82. When the strong Muse Khan had broken his lance, Goda, enraged, took up a sword shaped like the belly of a black crow.

83. Wielding that lightning of a sword, he cut down Pathans there by the hundred, and they fell to the ground in pieces.

84. But Muse Khan, seeing that superhuman deed of his, struck him on the left arm with his own sword.

85. Though struck on the arm by the swift Muse Khan, the great hero did not tremble, as a forest tree does not tremble in the wind.

86–87. Their brows knitted, their eyes very angry, their hands with their terrible creeper-like swords going and coming, roaring like elephants and striking each other, their helmets and armour shattered, the two of them shone.

88. Then, cut by each other's weapons, both together sprinkled the earth with streams of blood.

89. Ah! For a moment the battle between those two was even; then, through the wound he had been dealt, Goda gained the upper hand.

90. Then, though shaken by many blows, the strong Muse Khan brought his sword down on Goda's head.

91. Before his sword could fall on Goda's head, Goda struck that enemy with his own sword.

92. Struck then by Goda, wrath in bodily form, he was split from the shoulder to the middle into two halves.

93. His body, cut in two, fell, and the ground turned red with the gushing of his blood.

94. When Muse Khan had been struck down by the scion of the Jagtap family, who set the world right, Muslims by the hundred became guests of Yama there.

95. Unable to fight Sado sword against sword, the enemy drew back and, out of pride, took up a fine bow.

96–97. Then Sharfa Shah struck him with sharp arrows, drawing a bow that bent into a ring to the edge of the horizon, many-coloured like the bow of Indra. Sado, sword in hand, came at him and cut the bow.

98–99. When his quick-handed foe had cut in two that bow, bright with many colours and set with silver points, he threw away the useless bow and at once took up a terrible iron club to kill him.

100. Sado, roaring like a lion, cut that weapon of his the moment he had taken hold of it.

101. Whatever weapon the Muslim took in his hand, Sado, in the heat of heroism, cut it at once.

102. Then, weaponless, turning his face away, the Muslim took the road of flight as best he could.

103. Then the Ghatge too, after fighting that enemy warrior for a long time, quickly fled from him, as one elephant flees another.

104. Mina and Ratan too, taking Bhairav for Bhairava himself, slipped away from that battle, which was like the end of the world.

105. Fateh Khan did not look at his army as it turned back from Purandar seeking safety; he lost heart and turned his face away.

106. When the son of King Shah had broken by force in battle Fateh Khan, who had come against him with a great army, the hero, his course unchecked through the favour of fate, his valour ever rising, alas, turned to conquer the lord of the earth at Vijayapura.

107. Badly beaten in battle by King Shiva, Fateh Khan in dejection went back to Vijayapura. Hearing that news in the assembly, Mahmud at once sank, his mind long tormented, in an ocean of anxiety.

*Thus ends the fourteenth canto, called "The Killing of Muse Khan", in the Samhita, the Anupurana brought to light by Kavindra Paramananda.*
