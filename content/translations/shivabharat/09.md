# Canto 9

*The prince of poets said:*

1–2. Then, when the lord of Delhi, having gained Devagiri, was glad at heart, and the mad Mahmud was dejected because his army had been routed, King Shah quickly brought under his control by force many of the Nizam Shah's mountain forts, Shivneri first among them.

3–4. Overrunning step by step the whole country lying along the most holy Godavari, the mighty Pravara, the Nira with water like the ocean of milk, and the fearsome Bhimarathi, he quickly brought the Sahya mountains too under his control.

5–7. When he stood against the lord of Delhi, as against Indra, the Maharashtra kings came over to this great king: the Ghatges, the Kantes, the Gaikwads, the Kanks, the Dhomares, the Chavans, the Mohites, the Mahadiks, the Kharates, the Pandhares, the Waghs, the Ghorpades and others. King Shah made them captains of his army.

8. Then the son of Jahangir, wishing to conquer Shah, at once made a treaty with Adil Shah.

9. Then, as those two sought to conquer King Shah, the king of kings, in war, the great river named Bhima became the boundary between them.

*The learned men said:*

10–11. For how many years did the bold King Shah, valiant as a lion, fight with the armies of Shahjahan and of Adil? And how did the lord make peace with both of them? Prince of poets, we wish to hear this from you.

*The prince of poets said:*

12. Shah, of a thousand-rayed sun's valour, fought the armies of Shahjahan and Adil for three years.

13. Then the matted-haired god, lord of dreams, came to him in a dream, and as Shah bowed to him he spoke, lighting up the quarters with the gleam of his teeth:

*The matted-haired god said:*

14. "This lord of Delhi, of great splendour, is very hard to conquer in the world. So stop this unending fighting, wise one.

15. "Because of the fierce austerity this wicked man once performed, he will not be destroyed until it is exhausted.

16. "All these Muslims are of the line of the elder gods, the demons, and at every step they hate the gods and the gods on earth, the Brahmins.

17. "The one who has come down to earth to kill these Muslims is the blessed Vishnu himself, your child named Shiva.

18. "He will soon do what you have in mind. So wait a while, strong-armed one."

19. When the god with matted locks, pleased, had spoken thus, the lord of the earth woke at dawn with a clear mind.

20. Then Shah gave the Nizam's country, except his own territory, partly to the lord of Delhi and partly to Adil.

21. Though stubborn by nature, Shah, at the command of the three-eyed god, gave up his stubbornness and made peace with Adil and the copper-faced men.

22–25. Then, when the copper-faced men, having gained the Nizam's country and being glad at heart, had withdrawn to their own lands after their show of strength, the very wise Adil, thinking himself weak, reflected in his heart: "These copper-faced men, who sank the Nizam, a breaker of enemy armies, in battle, will probably sink me too. So I shall place this strong-armed king, son of Maloji, in my service, and do what I wish.

26–28. "Formerly my father Ibrahim, relying on his strength, destroyed his enemies and was free of fear. But after he went to heaven I foolishly slighted him, and he left me and went away. This great and proud king, strong-armed, was honoured by Ibrahim more than by me and raised with affection."

29. Thinking this over in his mind, the very splendid Mahmud at once sent ministers to King Shah.

30. Persuaded there by those ministers, skilled in counsel, the strong-armed one promised to help Adil.

31. Then Mahmud, having gained King Shah's support, had great joy at every step.

32–33. Then the valiant Mahmud sent Randulla, son of Farad Khan, a man able to bear the burden of war, dear to the whole army and foremost of generals, together with the strong great King Shah, to conquer the Karnataka region.

34–35. Then Randulla set out with Farad Khan, Yakut Khan, Ankush Khan, Husain, Ambar Khan and Masud, and with fine warriors — the Pawars, Ghatges, Ingles, Gades, Ghorpades and others.

36. The great-minded, strong King Bhosle went with that general to the Karnataka country.

37–42. The valiant Shah brought under his control Virabhadra, the very powerful lord of Bindupura; the famous Kenga Nayaka, ruler of Vrishapattana; Jagadeva, strong-armed lord of Kaveripattana; the cruel Kanthirava, lord of Shrirangapattana; the hero Vijayaraghava, lord of Tanjapura; the bold Venkata Nayaka of Tanji; the proud Trimalla Nayaka of Madhura; the arrogant Venkata, ruler of the Pilugunda region; the steady Shriranga Raja, lord of Vidyanagara; the famous Tammagauda, lord of Hamsakuta; and other kings besides. He pleased the general Randulla.

43–44. Then, after a war that slaughtered heroes by night and day, the victorious king lived in the city called Bengrul, very lovely, which had been taken from the warlike Kempagauda and given to him by Randulla as a reward.

45–55. That excellent king, equal to Vasava, lived happily in many ways with his own people in that fine city: with its strong walls and gateways; its white plastered mansions whose banners scraped the sky; its interiors filled with the lovely houses of craftsmen and artists; its roofs and ledges cooing with dove-pairs perched on them; adorned with the cries of peacocks flying up from the windows; its broad bazaars stocked with goods for sale; with wells of glittering water at every step and long step-wells in bloom; its lakes bright with many lilies; its grounds made fine with clusters of flowering trees; with walls on which bright pictures delighted the eye; with lovely rows of houses of many colours joined together; adorned with gates, market-squares, roofs and floors of opened stone; guarded on all sides by expert armies; shining with a moat full of water reaching the underworld; fenced with a rampart like a boundless ocean; with sweet bowers waving in the breeze; and filled with temples whose golden finials shone. Sometimes he went hunting, sometimes he waited on holy men, sometimes he worshipped Shambhu, and sometimes he discussed poetry.

56–59. Sometimes he took pleasure in watching dancing girls dance; sometimes he practised drawing the bow in many ways; sometimes he inspected the weapons laid out in the armoury; sometimes he reviewed the troops he had gathered; sometimes he made excursions to the city gardens, fragrant with flowers; sometimes he enjoyed the pleasures of love with doe-eyed women; sometimes he practised yogic postures according to the art taught in the shastras of yoga. So the lord passed his time, each pleasure in its season.

60. There, though the inner apartments held many lovely women, the mother of Shambhu and Shiva, the daughter of the Yadava, held her husband's heart.

61. As Vasudeva shone with Halin and Hari, so Shah shone with Shambhu and Shiva.

62. The great king loved his son Shiva greatly, younger than Shambhu but greater in the wealth of his virtues.

63. From the day that son Shiva was born, all Shah's riches grew.

64. Many elephants stood at his door, lovely as Mount Mandara, their temples outshining the lotus, pouring out the water of rut.

65. In his stables were thousands of lovely Sindhi horses, swift as the wind, unstoppable in battle.

66. His treasury grew daily, and with it his anger; his valour and power increased day by day.

67. Forts hard to take became easy for him; he was always victorious; not even in a dream did he know defeat.

68. Flowers, fruits and crops increased, and all the successes he wished for came to him even without effort.

69. Raised so high by that son, the soul of the Primal Person, the son of Malaji rejoiced greatly.

70. Then, seeing that his son, endowed with virtues, was seven years old, the king thought: "He is fit to learn his letters."

71. He seated his son, intelligent and clear of speech, on his teacher's lap together with the ministers' sons of his own age.

72. Before the teacher had finished telling him to write the first letter, he had written the second as well and showed it.

73. The teacher made him learn the whole of the script, which is the doorway to all learning.

74. The teacher, his mind gratified, seeing that lion-cub of a king — naturally intelligent, of good temper, of a power beyond words — learn the script so fast, felt in the gathering of the learned that this was something quite out of the ordinary.

*Thus ends the ninth canto in the Samhita, the Anupurana brought to light by Paramananda of Nivasa.*
