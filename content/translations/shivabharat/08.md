# Canto 8

*The learned men said:*

1. You have told us, Paramananda, that King Shiva was born on the hill of Shivneri. We have a doubt about this.

2–3. For that hill, very famous, valiant and renowned in the world as a second Dharagiri, was the Nizam's and served his interest. How did King Shah come there? Tell us everything, son of Bhatta Govinda.

*The prince of poets said:*

4. Listen, best of the twice-born, to this story of King Shah, sweeter than nectar and holy.

5–8. When Ambar, the Abyssinian, bright as the sun, had set, the Nizam, left without a minister, grew doubtful about his own survival. When by fate Ibrahim too, who had been his match, went to heaven, and his son Mahmud took his place; and when the armies of Shahjahan, who had become lord of Delhi, came proudly to the southern quarter to seize it; then the strong-armed King Shah, wishing to help the Nizam because of their old ties, left the city called Vijaya.

9. Jadhavrao too left the copper-faced men, whom he had followed, and came over to Dharagiri, taking the Nizam's side.

10–11. Meanwhile, Vijayraj, son of Siddhapal, of the line of Vishvasrao, greatly trusted by the Nizam, a devotee of Shiva, of great vows, famous, very wealthy, lived as a king on the hill of Shivneri.

12. He thought his daughter Jayanti a fit match for the excellent Shambhuraja, son of King Shah.

13. Thinking that alliance most praiseworthy on earth, King Shah too asked for Jayanti as a daughter-in-law.

14. Then, as befitted the alliance, a great festival was held between the two of them, Vijayraj and King Shah.

15. A great gathering took place of the kings of Vishvasrao's line, of great energy, and of the Bhosles.

16. The great wedding of Jayanti and Shambhuraja, a pair with qualities worth praising, was held there at Shivneri.

17–18. Then, after some days, when the great festival was over, King Shah, with his kinsman's consent, left his pregnant wife there on top of that very hill with his own people, and set out, wishing to conquer Daryakhan.

19. So I have told you how King Shah came to be at Shivneri. What else do you wish to hear?

*The learned men said:*

20–21. When the great King Jadhavrao, of great vows and strong arms, had suddenly left the lord of Delhi and come over to the Nizam's side, and was ready to fight the copper-faced men, who were attacking, what did the Nizam do to get what he wanted?

*The prince of poets said:*

22. Then, alas, as fate would have it, the mind of the Nizam, whose heart was set on pleasures, was turned the wrong way by the wicked ministers who served him.

23. To that madman the bad seemed good, and the good, even a man of sweet speech, seemed bad.

24. With his perverted sight he made light of his elders, and soon lost his respect even for elders rich in virtue.

25. Alas, the great kingdom of that fickle-minded madman, a drinker of wine and a speaker of what should not be spoken, went to ruin.

26. Then, when the very splendid lord of the Yadavas came to pay his respects, the evil-minded Nizam insulted him.

27. Insulted by the Nizam, the great and proud lord of the Yadavas then flew into a heroic rage.

28–29. Then the wicked generals, Hamid and the others, primed beforehand by the Nizam's evil counsel, surrounded the lord of the Yadavas at the edge of the gateway of the hall as he was turning back, proud as a maddened elephant.

30. There, fighting many of them, with his sons, ministers and kin, he was welcomed by hosts of gods and gazed on the world of the gods.

31–32. Like the toppling of Meru, the fall of the sun, the death of Death himself or the burning of the lord of the waters, the end of the lord of the Yadavas there was, alas, a calamity for all the seven worlds.

33. Hearing of that fate of his father-in-law, the lord of the Yadavas, the famous King Shah gave up helping the Nizam.

34. Then the army of the copper-faced men quickly crossed the Tapi and laid siege to Dharagiri, held by the Nizam.

35. At that moment the very proud and greedy Adil gathered his own army and sent it towards Dharagiri.

36. Shahjahan's army and Mahmud's fought each other there day after day, each wanting to take Dharagiri.

37. The Nizam Shah himself, standing on the summit of Dharagiri, then fought both those armies.

38. Then the Nizam was quickly overwhelmed by the very strong armies of the copper-faced men and by Mahmud's armies, which fought on.

39–40. Then, with that fort, his army of many kinds, his foolish minister Fateh Khan, his whole household and his great treasure, he sank there in the ocean of the copper-faced men's army.

*The learned men said:*

41–45. He who had eighty thousand swift horses and eighty-four mountain forts, and many forts on land and in water; whose rich country was under his sway, unassailable by enemies; who at every step took alive the whole army of the proud Adil Shah and of the lord of Delhi; at whose sudden attack, as at the swoop of a falcon, enemy birds hid in terror — for what reason did that Nizam then perish? Tell us, prince of poets, for we wish to hear.

*The prince of poets said:*

46–47. When his father Ambar, who cared for all, had died, Fateh Khan, a man of little sense, by the force of what was to be, became the Nizam's minister and, valiant and pitiless as Death, tormented the people.

48–49. From the day the Nizam, on the counsel of the evil-minded Hamid, had the king of the Yadavas killed, all the kings, King Shah and the rest, and the Muslim soldiers too, grew sick at heart.

50–51. Distrustful, angry and frightened, some took refuge with Adil, some with the lord of Delhi; some cruel-hearted men turned to open hostility; and some, alas, made a show of standing aside.

52. Through the wickedness of that evil-minded man, terrible signs then appeared to bring about the destruction of living beings.

53. For a long time the rain did not fall on his country; grain became very hard to get, while gold became cheap.

54. Rich men gave jewels in exchange for a mere measure of grain and with great trouble obtained a few measures of horse-gram.

55. With nothing to eat, crying out in despair, beasts ate beasts and men, robbed of their senses, ate men.

56–57. Through that great drought, the attack of the enemy, and the lack of the old ministers and of a large army, the evil-minded Fateh Khan and the lord of Dharagiri, wasting away moment by moment, were caught by the copper-faced men, who followed in their footsteps.

58. When time is favourable, everything favours a man; when it turns against him, everything is against him.

59. For the embodied man to whom the blessed, eternal Time is favourable, all tasks succeed without effort.

60. Birth, existence, growth, ripening, decline and destruction: these six states are changes made by Time.

61–62. Victory and defeat, enmity, the strength or weakness of counsel, learning and ignorance, generosity and meanness, activity and rest, freedom and dependence, prosperity and adversity — all arise as Time turns.

63. Birth, life and death, those three states, come from Time, and so do sacrifices and other rites.

64–70. Without Time there is no seed, without Time no sprout; without Time no flower, without Time no fruit; without Time no holy place, without Time no austerity; without Time no success, without Time no victory. Without Time the moon, fire and sun do not shine; without Time the ocean does not rise and fall. Without Time Bhagiratha did not bring down the Ganga; without Time King Nriga was not freed from being a lizard; without Time Rama did not kill the ten-headed one; without Time Vibhishana did not gain Lanka; without Time Krishna did not hold up Govardhana; without Time Partha did not kill Vaikartana. Time alone is the cause of joys and sorrows. I believe Time is the Lord, maker of creation, preservation and destruction.

71–72. Broken in battle, the Nizam perished; the demon lord of Delhi gained Devagiri and rejoiced; and Adil Shah too was put to shame by the rout of his army. Know, Brahmins, that all of this came about through Time.

73. When the copper-faced men had blockaded Dharagiri, a fort hard to take, and seized the Nizam there, Adil's army, its pride gone, withdrew, they say.

*Thus ends the eighth canto in the Anupurana composed by Kavindra Paramananda.*
